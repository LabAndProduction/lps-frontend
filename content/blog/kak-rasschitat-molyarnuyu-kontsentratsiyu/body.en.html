To calculate molar concentration, divide the amount of solute in moles by the volume of the solution in liters: C = n / V. If you're given mass, first find n = m / M, where M is the molar mass from the periodic table. For example, 5.85 g of NaCl in 250 mL of solution is 0.1001 mol per 0.250 L, which is 0.400 mol/L. Below you'll find how to calculate molar concentration in typical textbook problems, how to convert mmol/L and mg/L, and how molarity differs from molality.

What Is Molar Concentration?

This quantity has one formula, and all other calculations are derived from it. Molar concentration tells you how many moles of a substance are present in one liter of solution:

C = n / V

  • C – molar concentration, mol/L (often written as M: "0.1 M solution" means 0.1 mol/L);
  • n – amount of substance, mol;
  • V – volume of the entire solution (not the solvent), L.

Textbooks sometimes use the notations c, CM, and square brackets: [H⁺] = 0.01 mol/L. The strict definition of molar concentration in SI is given in mol/m³, but in the lab you almost always work with mol/L and mmol/L. 1 mol/m³ = 0.001 mol/L = 1 mmol/L.

Two other formulas needed for problems follow from the same equation:

  • n = C · V – how many moles are in a given volume;
  • V = n / C – what volume of solution contains the required amount of substance.

The key rule is volume in liters. If the volume is given in milliliters, divide by 1000: 250 mL = 0.250 L, 75 mL = 0.075 L.

Molar Mass and Amount of Substance – Where n Comes From

Problem statements rarely give the amount of substance directly. Usually the mass of the sample is known, and then the question of how to find the amount of substance in a solution is answered by the second formula:

n = m / M

  • m – mass of the substance, g;
  • M – molar mass, g/mol. It is calculated as the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in the formula.

Molar mass and amount of substance are related the same way as price per item and number of items: mass is the "total", molar mass is the "price per mole". Combining the two formulas gives the working formula for calculating from a weighed sample:

C = m / (M · V)

If you're working with real reagents and standards, it's convenient to take the molar mass directly from the packaging.

Worked Molar Concentration Problems

First the general procedure, then four problems: by mass, by amount of substance, with a hydrate, and with milligrams.

How to Calculate Molar Concentration from Mass – Step by Step

Here's how to calculate molar concentration in any problem where the mass of solute and the volume of solution are known:

  1. Write the formula of the substance and check whether it is anhydrous or a hydrate.
  2. Calculate the molar mass M, g/mol, to two decimal places.
  3. Find the amount of substance: n = m / M.
  4. Convert the solution volume to liters.
  5. Divide: C = n / V.
  6. Check the order of magnitude: textbook solutions usually fall in the range 0.001–2 mol/L. An answer of 0.0004 mol/L instead of 0.4 – a thousand times smaller than expected – almost always means the volume was left in milliliters.
  7. Round the answer according to the least precise input (usually 2–3 significant figures) and write the units.

Problem 1. Mass of Salt and Volume of Solution

Given. 5.85 g of NaCl was dissolved in a 250 mL volumetric flask and made up to the mark with water. Find the molar concentration.

Solution.

  1. M(NaCl) = 22.99 + 35.45 = 58.44 g/mol.
  2. n = 5.85 / 58.44 = 0.1001 mol.
  3. V = 250 mL = 0.250 L.
  4. C = 0.1001 / 0.250 = 0.4004 mol/L.

Answer. C(NaCl) ≈ 0.400 mol/L (0.400 M).

Problem 2. Amount of Substance Given

Given. 75 mL of solution contains 0.015 mol of HCl. What is the concentration?

Solution. n is already given; just convert the volume. V = 75 / 1000 = 0.075 L. C = 0.015 / 0.075 = 0.20 mol/L.

Answer. 0.20 mol/L – two significant figures, matching the given 0.015 mol and 75 mL.

Problem 3. Hydrate

Given. 12.48 g of copper sulfate pentahydrate CuSO₄·5H₂O was dissolved to make 500 mL of solution. Find the molar concentration of CuSO₄.

Solution.

  1. M(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = M(CuSO₄) + 5 · M(H₂O) = 159.607 + 5 · 18.015 = 159.607 + 90.075 = 249.68 g/mol.
  2. n = 12.48 / 249.68 = 0.04998 mol. One mole of the hydrate gives one mole of CuSO₄, so n(CuSO₄) = 0.04998 mol.
  3. C = 0.04998 / 0.500 = 0.09996 mol/L.

Answer. C(CuSO₄) ≈ 0.100 mol/L. If you mistakenly use the molar mass of the anhydrous salt, 159.61 g/mol, you get 0.156 mol/L – an overestimate by more than 1.5 times.

Problem 4. Milligrams and Milliliters

Given. 37.3 mg of KCl is dissolved in 50 mL of solution. Find the concentration in mol/L and mmol/L.

Solution. A handy trick: divide milligrams by molar mass to get millimoles. n = 37.3 / 74.55 = 0.5003 mmol. Then mmol per mL is exactly the same as mol per L: C = 0.5003 / 50 = 0.01001 mol/L.

Answer. 0.0100 mol/L, or 10.0 mmol/L.

If you're given grams and liters, calculate concentration in mol/L the same way, just without prefixes: divide grams by M to get moles, then divide moles by liters. The key is not to mix milligrams with liters in the same formula.

How to Find the Amount of Substance in a Solution from Known Concentration

The reverse problem comes up just as often: the concentration is known, and you need to find how much substance is in an aliquot. The answer to how to find the amount of substance in a solution comes from the same formula rearranged as n = C · V.

Example. How many moles and grams of NaCl are in 35 mL of a solution with C = 0.12 mol/L?

  1. V = 0.035 L.
  2. n = 0.12 · 0.035 = 0.0042 mol = 4.2 mmol.
  3. m = n · M = 0.0042 · 58.44 = 0.245 g ≈ 0.25 g.

The amount of substance doesn't change when you dilute with water, so n = C · V immediately gives the dilution rule C₁ · V₁ = C₂ · V₂. If 10 mL of 0.400 mol/L solution is diluted to 100 mL, the new concentration is C₂ = 0.400 · 10 / 100 = 0.0400 mol/L. You can leave the volumes in milliliters here – they appear on both sides of the equation and cancel out.

The same logic underlies titrimetry: from the volume of titrant of known concentration you find n, and then the concentration of the sample. That's why the accuracy of the answer depends on how well you can titrate correctly with a burette in practice – an error in volume carries straight through to concentration.

Converting mmol/L to mol/L and mg/L

In medicine, water analysis, and food testing, concentrations are more often given in mmol/L or mg/L. Converting mmol/L to mol/L and mg/L follows two simple rules, and converting back from mg/L uses the same steps in reverse:

  • mmol/L → mol/L: divide by 1000;
  • mmol/L → mg/L: multiply by the molar mass in g/mol (1 mmol of a substance weighs M milligrams).

Example 1. Blood glucose is 5.5 mmol/L. How much is that in mg/L and g/L? 5.5 · 180.16 = 990.9 mg/L ≈ 0.99 g/L. In mol/L it's 0.0055.

Example 2. Water contains 100 mg/L of calcium ions. How much is that in mmol/L and mol/L? 100 / 40.08 = 2.495 mmol/L ≈ 2.50 mmol/L, i.e., 0.00250 mol/L.

If the concentration is given in g/L, convert to mol/L by dividing by M: 23.4 g/L NaCl / 58.44 = 0.400 mol/L.

For ions, use the molar mass of the ion itself, not the salt it came from: if the problem says "mg/L of chloride ions", divide by 35.45, not 58.44.

How Molarity and Molality Differ

The difference is in the denominator, even though the names are nearly identical:

QuantityFormulaUnitsWhat's in the denominator
Molarity (molar concentration)C = n / V(solution)mol/Lvolume of the entire solution
Molalityb = n / m(solvent)mol/kgmass of solvent only

Molarity depends on temperature: when heated, the solution expands, volume increases, and C drops. Molality is temperature-independent, which is why it's used in calculations of boiling and freezing points of solutions. For dilute aqueous solutions (up to 0.01–0.05 mol/L), the numbers nearly coincide because a liter of such a solution contains about a kilogram of water. For concentrated solutions, they diverge noticeably.

If you need to figure out how to calculate molar concentration from known molality, you'll need the density of the solution.

Example. 58.44 g of NaCl (exactly 1 mol) was dissolved in 1000 g of water. The density of the resulting solution is 1.038 g/mL. Find the molality and molarity.

  1. Molality: b = 1.000 / 1.000 kg = 1.000 mol/kg.
  2. Mass of solution: 1000 + 58.44 = 1058.44 g.
  3. Volume of solution: V = 1058.44 / 1.038 = 1019.7 mL = 1.0197 L.
  4. Molarity: C = 1.000 / 1.0197 = 0.981 mol/L.

The difference is about 2% – already larger than the tolerance of volumetric glassware. So "1 mole per kilogram of water" and "1 mole per liter of solution" are different solutions, and it's important not to mix up mol/kg and mol/L in your answer.

Common Student Mistakes

Most mistakes aren't about not knowing how to calculate molar concentration, but about units and misreading the problem.

  • Volume in milliliters. 0.1001 mol / 250 = 0.0004 instead of 0.400. Checking the order of magnitude catches this immediately.
  • Solvent volume instead of solution volume. "Dissolved in 250 mL of water" and "made up to 250 mL" are different conditions. In the first case, the exact solution volume is unknown without density.
  • Hydrate without water. CuSO₄·5H₂O, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, MgSO₄·7H₂O – calculate molar mass including water of crystallization.
  • Mass of salt instead of mass of ion. In water and blood problems, concentration is often given for the ion. Molar mass and amount of substance are calculated for the particle the problem asks about.
  • Confusing molarity and normality. For H₂SO₄, 0.1 mol/L corresponds to 0.2 N in complete neutralization reactions. If the problem says "N" or "mol-eq/L", that's a different quantity.
  • Prefixes when converting units. When converting mmol/L to mol/L, people often multiply by 1000 instead of dividing. Quick check: in mol/L the number is always 1000 times smaller than in mmol/L. When converting to mg/L, multiply by molar mass, not by 1000.
  • Rounding too early mid-calculation. Keep intermediate values with one or two extra digits and round only the final answer.
  • No units in the answer. A number like 0.4 without "mol/L" won't be accepted on an exam.

What Glassware and Equipment You Need

Knowing how to calculate molar concentration isn't enough: the calculation is only as accurate as the mass and volume you plug into it. Molar mass and amount of substance come from the calculation, but the sample mass and solution volume come from the balance and volumetric flask, and that's where the error originates. For a teaching lab and a working laboratory, you'll need:

  • An analytical balance with 0.1 mg readability – for samples from tens of milligrams, such as the Sartorius Entris II analytical balance. On a technical balance with 0.01 g increments, a 0.05 g sample is weighed with up to 20% error.
  • A Class A volumetric flask – this is what sets V in the formula. For problems like the first one, a 250 mL Class A volumetric flask with a ground-glass joint works well.
  • A beaker, funnel, and wash bottle – to dissolve the sample and quantitatively transfer it to the flask.
  • Pipettes – for taking aliquots and making dilutions using C₁ · V₁ = C₂ · V₂.

Flask volume, accuracy class, and joint are chosen to match the procedure, and if you're buying a flask for your own lab, it's worth understanding in advance how to choose a volumetric flask for your typical solution volumes. A graduated cylinder and a beaker with markings won't do for precise concentrations: their error is several times larger than that of a volumetric flask.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate molar concentration if I only know the mass fraction?

You also need the density of the solution. Take 1 L of solution, find its mass, 1000 · ρ (ρ in g/mL), find the mass of the solute (mass of solution · w), then n = m / M — that gives you C in mol/L. Without density, the problem can't be solved.

How do I find the amount of substance in a solution if the volume is in mL and the concentration is in mmol/L?

Multiply and divide by 1000: n (mmol) = C (mmol/L) · V (mL) / 1000. For example, 20 mL of a 5 mmol/L solution contains 20 · 5 / 1000 = 0.1 mmol.

How do I calculate mol/L if the concentration is given in mg/L?

Divide mg/L by the molar mass — that gives you mmol/L, then divide by 1000. For example, 250 mg/L of sulfate ions SO₄²⁻ (M = 96.06 g/mol): 250 / 96.06 = 2.60 mmol/L = 0.00260 mol/L.

How do I calculate molar concentration after mixing two solutions?

You add the amounts of substance and the volumes, not the concentrations: C = (C₁V₁ + C₂V₂) / (V₁ + V₂). For example, 100 mL of 0.2 M and 100 mL of 0.4 M of the same substance give 0.3 M, not 0.6 M (volumes are assumed to be additive for dilute solutions).

Why does molarity depend on temperature?

Because volume is in the denominator, and it changes when heated. Volumetric glassware is calibrated at 20 °C, and the solution is made up to the mark at that temperature.

To understand how to calculate molar concentration in any problem, just keep the chain m → n → C in mind: mass divided by molar mass, moles divided by liters, and check the units at every step. Everything else — converting mmol/L to mol/L and mg/L, dilution, molality — follows from these same two formulas.
