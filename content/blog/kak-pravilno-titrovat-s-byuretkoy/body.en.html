How do you titrate correctly with a burette? The short answer: prepare a clean burette of the right class, fill it with titrant free of air bubbles, work the stopcock smoothly, take readings at the bottom of the meniscus at eye level, and control every drop near the equivalence point. Below we break down each step, with the details that affect the result.

Many people think titration is a simple operation: pour in the solution, open the stopcock, wait for the color to change. In practice, mistakes in preparing the glassware and taking readings cause discrepancies between replicate determinations, and the sample has to be redone. This article will help you set up your work so that results are reproducible and the technique of burette titration stops letting you down. Here's how to titrate correctly with a burette at every stage.

What a burette with a stopcock is and where it's used

A burette is volumetric glassware for precise dosing of titrant. A graduated glass tube with a stopcock lets you add solution in portions and record the volume that went into the reaction. In analytical practice, a burette serves for quantitative determination of concentration; in teaching, for mastering titration technique.

Burettes are used in chemical, pharmaceutical, food, and environmental laboratories. In Tashkent and across Uzbekistan, volumetric glassware is in demand in educational institutions, production sites, and research centers. For aggressive media, choose a burette with a PTFE stopcock; for standard aqueous solutions, a glass stopcock will do.

The burette works as a measuring instrument: its calibration and accuracy class directly affect the error of the analysis. So choose the item for the task, not "by eye."

Stopcock types and configurations: glass, PTFE, straight and side tube

The stopcock is the component on which smooth delivery depends. A glass stopcock provides good sealing with aqueous solutions but requires care and lubrication. A PTFE (Teflon) stopcock resists alkalis and many organic solvents, needs no lubrication, and jams less often.

By tube position, straight and side burettes are distinguished. A straight tube is convenient for standard stands; a side tube lets you work with wider vessels and reduces the risk of accidental contact. The choice depends on the layout of your workspace.

For teaching labs, burettes with a straight glass stopcock are often chosen: they're clear and easy to maintain. In analytical labs with a variety of solvents, a burette with a PTFE stopcock is more often selected. If in doubt, consult a specialist in laboratory volumetric glassware.

Accuracy classes and how to read burette markings

The burette body shows the nominal volume, accuracy class, calibration temperature (usually 20 °C), and the standard designation. Class 1 has a smaller error; class 2 has a wider tolerance. For teaching work, class 2 is sufficient; for quantitative analysis, choose class 1. For more on the differences, see the article Class A and B volumetric glassware.

Markings may include the manufacturer's trademark and country. If the burette is certified, the certificate gives corrections for temperature and calibration conditions. Don't ignore this data: under non-standard conditions, it helps you apply a correction. Requirements for burettes with a stopcock are set out in GOST 29251-91 (glass burettes) and ISO 385 (laboratory glassware, burettes) — check the markings against these documents.

Burette accuracy classes 1 and 2 differ in volume tolerance: the first is roughly twice as strict. Standard volumes are 10, 25, 50, and 100 mL; micro-burettes come in smaller sizes. Read the markings carefully: sometimes two scales are etched on the glass (e.g., for different modes), and they must not be confused.

Preparing the burette for titration: washing, filling, removing bubbles

Wash the burette before work. First rinse with distilled water, then with a small amount of titrant. Don't use detergents that leave a film: it affects wetting and readings. For washing, use mild solutions and a brush that won't scratch the glass. This preparation of the burette for titration takes 3–5 minutes and saves time on redoing the sample.

Fill the burette through a funnel, then remove the funnel so liquid from it doesn't get into the burette during titration. Fill the tube above the zero mark, open the stopcock, and quickly drain a little solution: this removes air bubbles from under the stopcock and from the tip.

If a bubble remains, tap the tube lightly with your finger or briefly open the stopcock fully. Make sure there's no drop at the tip. Set the level at zero or below and record the initial reading.

How to titrate correctly with a burette: delivery rate, mixing, working the stopcock

Titrate with constant mixing: swirl the flask gently without lifting it out from under the burette. At first, deliver the solution faster, but not in a jet—use a moderate stream. When color starts to appear and quickly disappear, slow down. The technique of burette titration is honed on replicates: the first sample almost always serves as a warm-up.

Near the equivalence point, add titrant drop by drop. Hold the stopcock so your fingers don't cover the scale. After each drop, mix and watch the color change. Add the last drop only after you've confirmed the color persists.

Don't interrupt titration for long: the solution in the tip may dry out and change concentration. Work at a steady pace, recording the volume on the scale after each stage. If you accidentally overshoot, start over: the result can no longer be considered reliable.

Reading the meniscus and parallax

For clear solutions, read the meniscus at its bottom edge. Your eye must be at the level of the meniscus: looking from above or below causes parallax error. For practice, use a card with a horizontal line, or clamp the burette so the scale is at eye level. It rarely comes out right the first time, so practice with water: correct meniscus reading comes with experience.

Record the volume to half a division. For example, if the division value is 0.1 mL, record 0.05 mL. Don't round during the process: calculate the final result after subtracting the initial reading.

If the solution is colored, the top meniscus may be easier to see. In that case, use the top edge and apply a correction according to the method instructions. The main thing is to choose one method and stick to it across all replicates.

Straight vs. side burette: which to choose for your workspace

Let's start with choosing a burette overall, because convenience of work depends on it. A straight burette stands vertically in a standard stand, the stopcock is at the bottom along the tube axis, and you work it with one hand. A side model has the stopcock on a side outlet: this is more convenient when there's a large conical flask or a flask with a magnetic stirrer under the burette. On teaching benches, straight and side burettes are often found side by side with titration setups where free access to the flask is needed.

For serial determinations with a stirrer, choose a side burette: the stream runs at an angle and doesn't hit the magnet. For quick single samples, a straight one is more convenient. Both have scales starting at the top, so the reading procedure is the same. If you're buying glassware in batches for 10–15 workstations, choose the same configuration: you won't have to retrain lab staff.

Burette calibration: how to check it without a metrologist

Do-it-yourself burette calibration boils down to checking volume with water. Fill the burette with distilled water to zero, then drain 5.00 mL portions into a pre-weighed flask with a ground stopper. Weigh each portion on a balance with a 0.001 g division, and record the mass in a table.

Calculate the volume using V = m / ρ, where ρ is the density of water at your temperature (at 20 °C it's 0.9982 g/mL). Compare the obtained volume with the nominal. For a class 1 burette of 50 mL, the permissible deviation at any point is about ±0.05 mL; for 25 mL, ±0.03 mL. If the deviation exceeds these limits, send the glassware for verification or replace it.

Carry out this check at least once a year, and also after changing a batch of titrant or repairing the stopcock. Keep a card for each burette: number, verification date, conditions. This way you'll see how the item behaves under intensive use and won't cast doubt on the result of an entire series.

Why the equivalence point doesn't coincide with the color change

The equivalence point is the moment when the amount of titrant chemically exactly matches the amount of analyte. We see the indicator's color change slightly later or earlier: each indicator has its own transition interval, and it doesn't perfectly match the equivalence point. For example, phenolphthalein changes color at pH 8.2–10.0, methyl orange at pH 3.1–4.4.

The practical consequence is simple: for weak acids and bases, choose the indicator based on the calculated pH at the equivalence point, not on the color you like. If the method requires a specific indicator, don't replace it with a "similar" one: an error of 0.05–0.1 mL accumulates unnoticed but translates into a percentage deviation.

A witness run helps reduce the indicator's contribution: titrate water with the same amount of indicator in parallel and subtract the consumed volume from the result. For precise work with a small aliquot, this correction is mandatory.

Laboratory titration of non-standard samples: turbid solutions and strong dilutions

Turbid or highly diluted samples require adjustments in technique. If the solution is turbid, the bottom meniscus isn't visible: place a white or black contrasting screen behind the flask and work by the meniscus edge against the light. A colored solution in a thick layer can completely hide the boundary, so halve the aliquot and dilute it with distilled water.

When diluting, the titrant volume decreases and the drop's share grows. Rule of thumb: if the calculated volume is less than 1 mL, switch to a 5 or 10 mL micro-burette with a division value of 0.01–0.02 mL. Laboratory titration of such a sample will give a far smaller error than recalculating a large volume.

Highly diluted samples are titrated with preliminary concentration: evaporate part of the sample in a water bath at 60–70 °C, cool, and titrate. Don't evaporate to dryness: losses from spattering will eat up the advantage of the concentrate.

Laboratory titration: organizing a workspace that saves time

The workspace affects speed and accuracy more than it seems. Position the burette so the scale is at eye level from your stool: then you won't have to stretch or stand up to take a reading. Keep distilled water, a pipette for the aliquot, the indicator, and the log within reach—every extra step aside turns into 5–10 extra minutes across 10 samples.

Lighting over the work area should be diffuse and from the side: a direct beam from a lamp over the flask creates glare on the scale and makes the meniscus hard to see. Place the magnetic stirrer to the left or right, but not directly under the tip if you're using a straight burette. Then a random bump of the stopcock with your elbow won't throw off the reading.

For a series of samples, set up a tray for 20 test tubes: number the prepared aliquots and titrate in order. This order reduces the chance of mixing up samples, and recording is done sequentially without jumping around.

What precision costs: where you can and can't cut corners on volumetric glassware

You can save on the configuration for teaching work: a class 2 50 mL burette costs noticeably less than a class 1, and it's sufficient for practicing technique. You can't save on accuracy class for arbitration and acceptance methods: the difference in tolerance, ±0.05 vs. ±0.1 mL, directly shifts the study result.

The second reasonable compromise is the number of burettes. One burette per two workstations reduces the purchase but creates a queue and forces you to leave titrant in the tube for long periods. For a daily series of 15–20 samples, buy one per workstation: the costs will pay off on redoing samples alone.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

Titration mistakes are more often related to preparation and readings than to the reaction itself. Clients sometimes say "make it look nice," meaning a perfectly set zero, and end up with a systematic shift. Below is a table of common situations.

Care, storage, and when it's time to replace the burette

After work, drain the remaining titrant, rinse the burette with distilled water, and let it drip dry. For storage, clamp it vertically in a stand or place it in a case. Don't leave alkalis and aggressive solutions in the burette for long: they can damage the stopcock and glass.

A PTFE stopcock needs no lubrication but requires periodic leak checks. Lubricate a glass stopcock with a thin layer of special grease if the instructions call for it. If droplets appear, check how the stopcock seats and replace the seal if necessary.

It's time to replace the burette if the glass has chips, cracks, or deep scratches in the scale area. Loss of stopcock tightness that can't be fixed by adjustment is another reason. For precision work, use a burette with a valid certificate and calibration.

Titration error: where it comes from and how to calculate it

Titration error is the sum of the burette tolerance (±0.03–0.05 mL), the drop volume from the tip (0.02–0.05 mL), and temperature differences. For a typical 10.00 mL sample and about 8–10 mL of titrant, the total volume error is rarely below 0.1%, but with small volumes the drop's share rises sharply.

Work it out for your own case: if you used 0.8 mL of titrant and one drop added 0.05 mL, that's a 6% deviation right there. Such volumes call for microtitration with dropwise delivery and pre-dilution of the sample. For volumes over 5 mL, a drop accounts for less than 1%, and here reading accuracy takes priority.

What reduces random titration error isn't a stricter method but repeatability: run 3 parallel samples, titrating each from zero. The average of three converges more accurately than the best single result. Watch the liquid film on the flask wall—dried droplets don't wash off immediately when you swirl.

Calculating titrant volume and concentration: a worked example

A titration doesn't end when the color changes but when you've recorded the volume and calculated the result. Let's walk through a titrant volume calculation with a concrete example: determining acid in a solution with 0.1 M sodium hydroxide. Titrating 10.00 mL of sample took 8.45 mL of NaOH, with a titrant equivalent molar concentration C = 0.1000 mol/L.

The formula is simple: C(sample) = C(titrant) × V(titrant) / V(sample). Plugging in: 0.1000 × 8.45 / 10.00 = 0.0845 mol/L. If you need the acid mass in grams, multiply the concentration by the molar mass (36.46 g/mol for HCl) and by the volume in liters: 0.0845 × 36.46 × 0.010 = 0.0308 g, that is, 3.08 mg in 10 mL.

Two rules keep the calculation honest: measure the sample volume with a pipette, not a graduated cylinder (a 10 mL cylinder has an error up to 0.1 mL versus 0.02 mL for a pipette); check agreement with two parallel samples whose difference in titrant volume must not exceed 0.1 mL. If the difference is larger, find the cause before recalculating.

Round only at the last step. Keep four significant figures throughout the calculation, and report the final answer to the same precision as the input data. A burette with 0.1 mL graduations won't give a result accurate to the third decimal place: don't add extra digits. That keeps the titrant volume calculation transparent and verifiable.

Checklist: check yourself before a titration series

A series of 10–20 samples catches errors faster than a single determination: a systematic shift of 0.05 mL accumulates and ruins the whole series. Go through these points in 2–3 minutes before you start.

  • The burette is clamped vertically, the stopcock turns smoothly, and there are no leaks: run dry hands along the tip after closing the stopcock—no drop should appear.
  • The tip is filled with solution and bubbles under the stopcock are removed. One leftover bubble causes a volume underestimate of 0.02–0.1 mL.
  • The liquid level is set at the zero mark or slightly above, and the first reading is recorded before titration begins, not from memory afterward.
  • The titrant is fresh: the preparation date is recorded, and for alkalis the titer is checked at least once every 2 weeks, since NaOH absorbs CO₂ from the air.
  • The indicator is added in the right amount: 2–3 drops per 50–100 mL; excess shifts the equivalence point.
  • The first sample's result is fully recorded, including initial and final readings, before you start the second.

If the checklist is complete but parallel samples differ by more than 0.1 mL, don't recalculate the result: check the burette calibration, the distilled water quality, and the titrant's expiration date. For educational work, 2 parallel determinations suffice; for analytical and arbitration methods, at least 3, with the difference judged against the method's specification (often 0.2–0.5% relative deviation).

Keep a log with columns "date → titrant → weighed portion → V1 → V2 → average volume → result." A month later, such a log will show why one series came out biased and save you from repeating experiments. Frequently asked questions

What volume and accuracy class of burette should I choose for a teaching lab versus an analytical lab?

For teaching work, a 25 or 50 mL burette of accuracy class 2 is sufficient. For analytical determinations, choose class 1 and a volume that matches the method: most often 25 mL with 0.1 mL graduations.

How do I properly fill a burette and remove air bubbles under the stopcock?

Fill the burette through a funnel above the zero mark, remove the funnel, open the stopcock, and drain some solution. Bubbles come out with the flow. If a bubble remains, tap the tube or open the stopcock sharply.

Why is the stopcock dripping, and can I replace a PTFE stopcock myself?

Dripping is usually due to seal wear or a loose fit. A PTFE stopcock can often be replaced if the manufacturer supplies replacement assemblies. Follow the instructions to avoid damaging the glass; it's best to have a specialist replace the PTFE stopcock.

How should I wash and store a burette so I don't throw off the calibration?

Rinse with distilled water and titrant, and don't use abrasives. Store it upright or in a case, avoiding impacts. With proper care the calibration holds, but periodic verification is still required.
