Microwave digestion turns a sample into a solution in 20–40 minutes: 0.2–0.5 g of sample is treated with 8–10 mL of concentrated nitric acid (with hydrogen peroxide, hydrochloric acid, or hydrofluoric acid if needed), sealed in a fluoropolymer vessel, and heated by microwaves to 175–220 °C. In a sealed vessel the acid heats far above its boiling point, so organics oxidize quickly and completely, while volatile mercury, arsenic, and selenium stay inside and end up in the solution for AAS or ICP. Below: how to choose acids for your matrix, programs for food, soil, and pharmaceutical samples, the workflow, worked calculations, and the mistakes that cause element losses.

How microwave digestion works in a sealed vessel

Microwaves at 2450 MHz are absorbed by polar water and acid molecules, and the solution heats throughout its entire volume rather than from the wall, as on a hotplate. A vessel made of PTFE, PFA, or TFM is nearly transparent to microwaves and barely heats up itself. This is what microwave mineralization is: oxidation of the organic portion of the sample by acid at high temperature and pressure.

Temperature is where the real gain comes from. In an open beaker, 65–68% nitric acid boils at about 120 °C, and you can't get it hotter no matter how long it sits on the hotplate. In a sealed vessel the vapors can't escape, pressure builds, and the same acid heats to 180–220 °C. Oxidizing power rises sharply. A rough rule of chemical kinetics is that reaction rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C:

k₂ / k₁ ≈ 2^((t₂ - t₁) / 10), where t₁ and t₂ are temperatures in °C and k₁ and k₂ are reaction rates.

Going from 120 to 175 °C gives 2^5.5 ≈ 45. What wet ashing in a Kjeldahl flask takes several hours to do, a microwave takes 10–20 minutes of hold time. It's an estimate, but the order of magnitude is exactly right.

The second advantage is containment. Sealed high-pressure vessels release neither acid vapors nor volatile element species, and almost no laboratory dust gets into the sample. Acid consumption is 8–12 mL instead of 30–50 mL for open digestion, which also means a smaller contribution from reagent impurities to the blank.

Wet ashing, dry ashing, and microwave mineralization

There are three ways to get a solid sample into solution for metal measurement. Dry ashing in a muffle furnace at 450–550 °C suits major elements like calcium, magnesium, potassium, iron, and zinc, and it requires porcelain crucibles and dishes. Wet ashing in an open flask needs no expensive instrument but takes a long time. Microwave digestion is faster and preserves volatile elements.

Which acids to choose for acid digestion

Acid digestion is almost always built on concentrated nitric acid. It oxidizes organics to CO₂ and water and yields soluble nitrates of nearly all metals. Other reagents are added depending on the matrix and the elements being determined.

Sulfuric and perchloric acids are generally not used in sealed microwave vessels. Sulfuric acid boils at about 338 °C and quickly overheats the solution beyond the temperature limit for fluoropolymers, and sulfates precipitate lead, barium, and calcium. Perchloric acid with organics in a sealed volume is an explosion hazard.

Programs for food, soil, and pharmaceutical samples

A microwave mineralization program is a ramp to the target temperature over 5–15 minutes followed by a hold. A slow ramp is needed for samples that react vigorously (sugars, fats, alcohols) so that pressure doesn't outrun temperature. The table gives guidelines for vessels of about 100 mL; take exact values from your method and your instrument's manual.

For microwave leaching of soils and sediments, the sample is heated to 175 ± 5 °C in about 5.5 minutes and held until the end of the ten-minute cycle. It's important to understand that this is a leach, not a total digestion: the silicate base of the soil is not broken down by nitric acid, and a white or gray residue remains at the bottom, which is normal. Total content requires hydrofluoric acid.

Workflow — from weighing to volumetric flask

  1. Inspect the sealed high-pressure vessels: liner integrity, seal, membrane or pressure-relief valve. Clean vessels should run a blank program with 5–10 mL of nitric acid.
  2. Weigh the sample directly into the bottom of the vessel to 0.001 g. Particles on the walls or on the sealing surface cause leaks and charring.
  3. Under the fume hood, add nitric acid, rinsing the sample off the walls with it. For reactive samples (sugars, carbonates, fats), leave the vessel open for 15–30 minutes for the initial reaction, then add hydrogen peroxide and wait another 10–15 minutes.
  4. Close the vessels and load them into the rotor symmetrically. Always include a blank: the same reagents without sample. Don't mix vessel types or very different matrices in one cycle.
  5. Start the program. A system like the TOPEX+ microwave digestion system with KJ100 rotor controls heating via temperature and pressure sensors and reduces power if pressure approaches the limit.
  6. Cool the vessels to 40–50 °C, or to room temperature when determining mercury. Open under the fume hood, slowly, lifting the lid away from you: the gas phase is rich in NO₂.
  7. Quantitatively transfer the solution to a 25 or 50 mL volumetric flask made of polypropylene or PFA — or PFA or glass for mercury (if there's no HF in the solution) — rinsing the vessel and lid three times with deionized water. If mercury is in the task, add hydrochloric acid to 1–2% in the final solution.
  8. Make up to the mark and mix. Filter or centrifuge any insoluble residue. Measure the same day, especially for mercury.

Microwave digestion requires space under the fume hood for opening vessels, a balance with 0.1–1 mg readability, plastic volumetric ware, and acid dispensers. Fume hoods, dispensers, and other equipment for this work are in the general laboratory equipment section, while vessels and rotors are selected to match your instrument model.

Why mercury and arsenic are lost in open vessels

Losses of mercury and arsenic during digestion are the main reason heavy-metal labs are switching to sealed vessels. These elements have volatile forms that form right during oxidation.

  • Mercury. Organic mercury compounds are readily reduced to metallic Hg on heating, which evaporates even at room temperature. HgCl₂ is volatile with water and acid vapors. In an open flask boiling with nitric acid, a significant fraction of mercury escapes within a couple of hours, and in a muffle furnace at 500 °C, all of it does.
  • Arsenic. In the presence of chlorides (salt in the product, added hydrochloric acid), arsenic converts to the trichloride AsCl₃ with a boiling point of about 130 °C and leaves with the vapors. Dry ashing loses both the arsenic itself and its oxide.
  • Selenium and antimony behave similarly: volatile chlorides and oxides, losses in open vessels.

Sealed high-pressure vessels don't let these forms escape: after cooling they condense and redissolve in the acid. Hence two rules. Open the vessel only when cold, otherwise part of the mercury leaves with the first puff of gas. Stabilize mercury solutions with hydrochloric acid or a small gold addition, and don't store them in polyethylene or polypropylene, to whose walls mercury adsorbs.

There's another subtlety with arsenic in fish. Most of it is present as arsenobetaine, which nitric acid at 180–200 °C does not fully break down. For ICP-MS this is not a problem — the instrument sees the arsenic atom in any form. But for hydride-generation AAS, undecomposed organic forms produce almost no signal, and the result comes out low. A more aggressive program or additional oxidation before reduction is needed. That's why acid digestion of fish samples for the hydride method should be verified against a certified reference material with a known arsenic content.

Sub-boiling acid purification and the blank

When determining trace lead, cadmium, and arsenic, impurities in the reagents themselves can easily produce a signal comparable to the sample's. Sub-boiling acid purification solves this: an infrared heater warms only the acid surface below the boiling point, vapors condense on a cold surface and run into the receiver. There are no bubbles, no droplets carrying metal salts, and only vapors pass into the distillate. This purification is done in fluoropolymer or quartz equipment, and the S1 acid purification system is designed specifically for it.

Example 1. Acid contribution to the blank. Digestion used 10 mL of nitric acid with a density of 1.391 g/mL containing 5 µg/kg lead. The solution is made up to 50 mL. The sample signal corresponds to 3.5 µg/L.

  • Acid mass: 10 mL · 1.391 g/mL = 13.91 g = 0.01391 kg.
  • Lead from the acid: 0.01391 kg · 5 µg/kg = 0.0696 µg.
  • Concentration in solution: 0.0696 µg / 0.050 L = 1.39 µg/L.
  • Share of the sample signal: 1.39 / 3.5 · 100% ≈ 40%.

Answer: nearly 40% of the signal comes from the acid itself. If it's purified to 0.05 µg/kg lead, the contribution drops to 0.014 µg/L, i.e., to 0.4% of the signal. The blank is still run every cycle and subtracted, and acid digestion for trace levels is done only with purified acid.

Calculating results and sample weight — worked examples

Once microwave digestion is complete and the solution is made up to the mark, the mass fraction of the element in the sample is calculated from the concentration in solution:

w = (Cx - Cb) · V / m

where w is the element content in the sample, µg/kg; Cx is the concentration in the sample solution, µg/L; Cb is the concentration in the blank solution, µg/L; V is the volumetric flask volume, mL; m is the sample weight, g. The units work out: µg/L · mL / g = µg/kg. If the solution was further diluted before measurement, multiply the result by the dilution factor.

Example 2. Lead in rice. Sample weight 0.500 g, solution made up to 50 mL. On a novAA 800 atomic absorption spectrometer in graphite furnace mode, Cx = 3.5 µg/L and the blank Cb = 0.5 µg/L.

Solution: w = (3.5 - 0.5) · 50 / 0.500 = 3.0 · 100 = 300 µg/kg.

Answer: 300 µg/kg, or 0.30 mg/kg.

Example 3. Is the sensitivity enough? The method's limit of quantification (LOQ) is based on a solution of 1 µg/L, final volume 25 mL. You need to monitor a level of 20 µg/kg in the sample.

  • With a 0.25 g sample: 1 · 25 / 0.25 = 100 µg/kg.
  • With a 0.5 g sample: 1 · 25 / 0.5 = 50 µg/kg.
  • For the LOQ, recalculated to the sample, to at least match 20 µg/kg, you need a sample mass of m = 1 · 25 / 20 = 1.25 g.

In practice the LOQ should be 2-5 times below the regulatory limit, i.e. 4-10 µg/kg, so even 1.25 g would be cutting it close.

Answer: you can't put 1.25 g of organic sample into a single vessel (why - see Example 4). You can do it once the acid digestion is complete: evaporate the solution almost to dryness and bring the residue up to 10 mL: with 0.5 g this gives 1 · 10 / 0.5 = 20 µg/kg, which again is just the regulatory level, and mercury and arsenic are lost during evaporation. So you need a more sensitive method (graphite furnace, hydride generation, ICP-MS) with a solution LOQ of 0.1-0.2 µg/L: with 0.5 g and 25 mL this gives 0.1 · 25 / 0.5 = 5 and 0.2 · 25 / 0.5 = 10 µg/kg.

Example 4. Where the pressure comes from. Why is microwave sample preparation of food samples limited to a 0.5 g sample? Take 0.5 g of a dry product with 45 % carbon in a vessel with a free volume of 90 mL at 200 °C.

  • Carbon: 0.5 · 0.45 / 12.011 = 0.0187 mol, and the same amount of CO₂ is formed.
  • Pressure of the CO₂ alone from the equation p = nRT / V: 0.0187 · 8.314 · 473.15 / (90 · 10⁻⁶) ≈ 8.2 · 10⁵ Pa = 8.2 bar.
  • According to the reaction C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂ + 4NO₂ + 2H₂O, each mole of CO₂ accounts for up to 4 moles of NO₂. Total gas up to 5 · 0.0187 = 0.094 mol, pressure up to 41 bar.
  • On top of that comes the vapor pressure: for water alone at 200 °C it is about 15.5 bar.

Answer: this is an upper estimate (some of the NO₂ dissolves and is oxidized again), but the main point is clear. The pressure grows in proportion to the organic sample mass, and with 1 g of product it doubles. That's why fatty and sugary samples are taken even smaller and heated in steps.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Sample too large. The pressure relief triggers and some of the solution and volatile elements is lost. Stick to 0.5 g for dry organics and 0.25 g for fats.
  • Hydrogen peroxide added right after weighing. A violent reaction before the vessel is even closed. Start with nitric acid and a pre-reaction, then add peroxide.
  • Sample on the seal. Leakage, charring, vessel damage. Put the sample on the bottom and rinse the walls with acid.
  • Opening warm vessels. Losses of mercury and arsenic, NO₂ in your face. Wait until they cool to 40-50 °C, and to room temperature when determining mercury.
  • Glass volumetric ware for solutions with HF. Glass dissolves, and the background for silicon, boron and sodium rises. Use only polypropylene or PFA.
  • No blank in each cycle. Acid impurities and vessel memory get recorded in the sample result.
  • Poor vessel cleaning after a rich sample. Memory effect for mercury and lead. After high concentrations, run a blank program with nitric acid.
  • Storing the mercury solution for a week. Mercury adsorbs on the walls and is reduced. Measure on the day of digestion.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the solution yellow after digestion?

The yellow color comes from dissolved nitrogen oxides or undestroyed organics. The nitrogen oxides go away if you leave the solution open for 10-15 minutes under the fume hood or purge it. If the solution stays yellow, cloudy or has a greasy film, the digestion is incomplete: reduce the sample mass, add hydrogen peroxide or raise the holding temperature by 10-20 °C.

Can you digest a sample with hydrogen peroxide alone?

No. Without acid, metals don't convert to a stable soluble form, and peroxide decomposes quickly. It is used only as an additive to nitric acid.

What acidity is needed in the final solution?

If 10 mL of 65 % nitric acid is brought up to 50 mL, you get about 2.9 mol/L HNO₃, not counting what was consumed in oxidation. For flame AAS and ICP-OES this is acceptable if the calibration solutions are prepared in the same acid. For ICP-MS the solution is usually diluted another 5-10 times, to 2-4 % nitric acid.

How is microwave sample preparation better than a heating block with closed tubes?

A block with screw-cap tubes runs at about 95-130 °C, essentially wet ashing at a moderate temperature, and for many extracts it is sufficient. But that temperature is too low for complete destruction of fats, proteins and persistent organics, and digestion takes hours. Microwave digestion runs at 175-220 °C, the oven reaches setpoint in minutes and monitors pressure in each cycle.

If you remember one thing: closed-vessel microwave digestion is essential when you determine mercury, arsenic, selenium or trace levels of metals. Match the acids to the matrix, keep the organic sample mass within 0.5 g, run a blank in each cycle, open vessels only when cold, and for µg/kg levels include sub-boiling acid purification in the process.
