How do you wash laboratory glassware? The short answer: first remove sample residues and choose a detergent suited to the type of contamination, then wash, rinse thoroughly in water of decreasing hardness, and dry so that no streaks or dust remain inside. The order of operations matters more than the detergent itself: even a good solution won't help if the steps are mixed up.

Washing laboratory glassware affects results more than you might think. A film from an old reagent or a trace of alkali after a poor rinse skews the blank reading, and you start looking for errors in the method, the instrument, or the reagents. The impact of glassware cleanliness on analytical results becomes obvious at the very first control run.

Below we'll cover contamination by type, the step-by-step procedure, measuring glassware separately, glass and plastic, drying, common mistakes, and cleanliness checks you can do by hand.

Why Proper Washing Matters: Impact on Analytical Results

Glassware with residues from a previous sample carries microamounts of substances into the new analysis. For trace analysis this is critical: even a small carryover of the analyte distorts calibration and biases the result low or high.

The second problem is surface chemistry. Alkali on the walls neutralizes acidic titrant, acid interferes with precipitation, and an organic film adsorbs dyes and obscures the meniscus in measuring glassware.

The third factor is scratches. Abrasives and hot alkaline water degrade the smooth glass surface; reagents get trapped in microcracks, and cleaning becomes harder with each cycle. A cleaning approach based on soaking preserves the surface longer than mechanical scrubbing.

The practical takeaway: the cleaning regime for laboratory glassware should be tailored to the analytical method, not to what's convenient. Trace analysis and preparative work require different approaches.

Glassware for trace analysis is best dedicated to one type of work and not mixed with general lab glassware.

Classifying Contamination and Choosing a Detergent for Each Type

There is no universal detergent. Classifying laboratory glassware contamination starts with the question: what is left on the walls — soluble salts, acid or alkali, organics, resins and fats, insoluble deposits, or metal traces.

Water-soluble salts and acids are removed with warm water and a mildly alkaline solution. Fats and organics — with surfactants or organic solvents. Resins and dried films require soaking and, if necessary, chromic acid cleaning solution — used with caution and in a fume hood.

Insoluble deposits (carbonates, metal oxides) are removed with acid, but not in measuring glassware. Metal residues and trace amounts are best subjected to acid soaking followed by repeated rinsing.

For standard procedures, ready-made laboratory concentrates are convenient: manufacturers provide application tables for each contamination type. For example, the Hellmanex and Decon lines specify working dilutions and temperatures for organics, inorganics, and traces. The detergent for laboratory glassware is also chosen based on whether it leaves a film: trace analysis requires formulations that rinse completely with deionized water.

The procedure is simple: identify the contamination type → choose a detergent for laboratory glassware → check compatibility with the glassware material → soak, don't scrub. Soaking laboratory glassware takes 1–3 hours and saves effort on the main wash.

Step-by-Step Washing: Pre-treatment, Main Wash, Rinsing

The procedure is the same for most cases. Only the detergent and the rigor of control differ. This is what the answer to "how to wash laboratory glassware" looks like in practice.

  1. Remove sample residues immediately after use: pour out the contents, rinse with water, don't let them dry.
  2. Soak the glassware in a warm detergent solution for the time specified by the manufacturer for that contamination type.
  3. Clean with a soft brush or bottle brush, avoiding abrasives and metal scouring pads.
  4. Rinse with tap water, then distilled water, then (for trace analysis) deionized water.
  5. Dry and check cleanliness before placing in a drying oven or on a rack.

Rinsing laboratory glassware proceeds in water of decreasing hardness: first tap water, then distilled, then deionized if needed. Each subsequent step removes residues from the previous one, so the water at the final stage needs changing more often than you'd think.

Choose the detergent based on the material: for plastic, temperature and solvents are limited by the manufacturer's instructions. If unsure about compatibility, check general laboratory equipment for washing and drying and confirm the regime with your supplier.

Specifics of Measuring Glassware: Flasks, Pipettes, Burettes, Cylinders

Never scrub the inside of measuring glassware: the calibrated surface gets damaged, and the graduation no longer matches the certificate. Washing measuring glassware means soaking and repeated rinsing, no brush inside the flask.

Flasks and cylinders are conveniently filled to the top with solution and left for a while, then emptied and rinsed. Pipettes and burettes require flow-through washing: the solution must pass through the channel, not stay in it.

Burette stopcocks are disassembled according to the manufacturer's instructions — not all models allow full disassembly. Lubricant is applied in a thin layer and only one compatible with the stopcock material.

Checking measuring glassware cleanliness is simple: water should drain as an even film, without droplets or breaks. Droplets indicate residual grease or surfactant.

If matte areas appear on the walls, the glassware has lost its class: for quantitative measurements it's better to replace it. How to choose a replacement taking accuracy class into account is covered in the article on Class A and B volumetric glassware.

Washing Borosilicate Glass and Plastic Glassware: Differences

Borosilicate glass withstands temperature changes and most acids except hydrofluoric. Therefore hot solutions and acid soaking are acceptable for it, but a sudden shift from hot to cold water is undesirable even for quality glass. Washing glass laboratory glassware is based on these properties.

Plastics (polypropylene, PMP, PTFE) are limited in temperature and sensitive to organic solvents. Acetone and chloroform can soften the surface, after which the glassware will accumulate contamination.

Washing plastic laboratory glassware relies on mild surfactants and warm water within the manufacturer's limits. Plastic volumetric items must not be scrubbed with abrasives: a cloudy surface distorts the reading.

Drying and Storage: Racks, Drying Ovens, Desiccators

Drying laboratory glassware is the stage where streaks and dust most often appear. If glassware is dried vertically in an open cabinet, water drains but dust gets inside.

A drying oven provides stable temperature and dust protection, but measuring glassware is placed in it with temperature limits in mind: overheating can alter calibration, and plastic deforms.

Racks and holders for flasks and test tubes are convenient for daily work. For trace analysis, after drying, glassware is covered and stored in a desiccator to avoid sorption of substances from the air.

  • Dry vertically, neck down.
  • Do not turn measuring glassware upside down on a hot surface.
  • Dry plastic below the temperature specified in its certificate.
  • Use closed cabinets or desiccators for storage.

Post-drying check: no streaks, droplets, or visible dust on the walls. Streaks mean poor rinsing or hard water.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Manual vs. Machine Washing

Manual washing suits small volumes and complex glassware: narrow-neck flasks, pipettes, burettes. It takes more time, but you control every step and see what's happening inside. Machine washing saves time on routine work but requires compatible glassware and validated programs.

Laboratory dishwashers run programs with set temperature and detergent dosing. A standard cycle takes 30–90 minutes depending on the program: pre-rinse, main wash, rinse, drying. The main limitation is compatibility: measuring glassware with fine graduation and low-temperature plastics should not go in the machine.

Manual washing is more often chosen by small labs and quality control departments with diverse glassware. Machine washing — by large analytical centers with a flow of similar glassware. The calculation is simple: if you process more than 100 items per day, the machine pays for itself through time savings and consistent results.

The phrase "I washed it with hot water and soap, seems clean" for a volumetric flask is a direct path to losing calibration. Soap leaves a film, hot water changes volume, and "seems clean" is not verified.

Another mistake: storing clean glassware next to chemicals. Vapors adsorb on the walls, and the glassware becomes dirty again. Keep it in a separate closed cabinet.

Checking Cleanliness and When to Replace Glassware

Checking laboratory glassware cleanliness takes minutes and requires no instruments.

  1. Wet the walls with distilled water: it should drain as an even film.
  2. Inspect against the light: streaks, matte areas, and droplets are signs of contamination.
  3. Run a blank: for trace analysis it will reveal carryover.
  4. Check the pH of the rinse with indicator paper if acid or alkali residues are suspected.

Glassware should be replaced if deep scratches appear, matte spots on the measuring part, chips on the neck or spout, or worn ground joints. Repairing such glassware is not worthwhile: the price of new is lower than the risk of an incorrect result.

How to Clean Glassware After Specific Contaminations: Case Studies

General rules work, but in practice people most often get stuck on a few typical situations. Below is what to do when glassware seems hopeless. It's also useful to know how to clean laboratory glassware in each specific case: there is no universal recipe.

Burned or Dried Organics

Let the glassware stand in a hot soda solution at 20–30 g per 1 L of water for 2–3 hours. Remove the softened layer with a soft brush. Do not scrape with a metal sponge: micro-scratches remain on the glass, and sample residues then accumulate in them. If the deposit doesn't come off, repeat the soaking, but do not increase the alkali concentration above 5% for measuring glassware.

Hardness salt traces and white film

White film is calcium and magnesium carbonates from tap water. Rinse it off with a 5–10% citric acid solution or diluted hydrochloric acid (1:10), then rinse thoroughly. For trace analysis, after the acid a double rinse with distilled water is a must: the acid itself becomes a contaminant.

Silver deposits and sulfides

Dark sulfide film is removed with a warm sodium thiosulfate solution (10–15%), while silver deposits on the walls come off with 1:1 nitric acid followed by neutralization and washing. Work with gloves and under a fume hood: acid vapors irritate the respiratory tract.

Plasticware after fats and oils

Hot water is acceptable up to 90 °C for polypropylene and up to 70 °C for polystyrene. Above that the plastic deforms and the vessel loses its geometry. Remove fats with a warm soapy solution plus 5 g of baking soda per 1 L. Abrasives leave scratches, so use a foam sponge.

How to wash labware when working with biological samples

After blood, serum, microbiological cultures and cell suspensions, the order changes: disinfect first, wash second. Autoclave the vessels at 121 °C for 20–60 minutes or fill them with a disinfectant solution per the instructions, and only then wash. Never pour undisinfected residues down the sink.

Protein contamination is hard to remove with alkali once it dries. Soak immediately in a warm solution with enzymatic components: proteases break down the protein film in 30–60 minutes. For plastic bottles and plates, enzymatic products are more convenient than alkaline ones because they work at 40–50 °C and don't soften the surface.

After disinfection and washing, vessels for culture work are additionally rinsed with deionized water and sterilized by dry heat at 160–180 °C or in an autoclave. Don't subject graduated volumetric ware to dry heat above 160 °C: the calibration will drift.

What washing costs: where a lab's expenses come from

There's usually no direct rate for washing labware, but the costs can be calculated. Take three components: the technician's labor, water and supplies, and wear on the glassware. For a rough estimate, use the formula: batch × time per unit × hourly cost + product consumption + losses from breakage and damage.

Example calculation for a batch of 50 flasks. Manual washing with soaking takes on average 2–3 minutes per unit, that is, 2–2.5 hours of technician time. At a nominal hourly cost of 30,000 sum, that comes to 60,000–75,000 sum. A soaking concentrate diluted to 1% yields roughly 0.5–1 L of working solution per 10 flasks, that is, 5–10 liters per batch. Rinse water adds another 30–50 liters, counting all three stages.

Machine washing a batch takes a 30–90 minute cycle plus loading and unloading. A machine saves labor but uses electricity and requires compatible glassware. What's worth calculating isn't the cost of a single wash but the cost of a ruined analysis: redoing a series with reagent prep and recalibrating the instrument usually costs more than a month's supply of detergent.

Checklist for the washing station

Hang this list by the sink: it saves time spent deliberating and reduces the risk of mixing up products. To know how to wash labware without mistakes, just check it before every batch.

  • Before washing: pour out sample residues, remove labels, put on gloves and goggles.
  • Choosing a product: acid for salts and film, alkali for organics and fats, solvent for resins and oils.
  • Temperature: no higher than 60 °C for volumetric ware, and per the spec sheet for heat-resistant glass.
  • Rinsing: tap water → distilled → deionized (for trace analysis).
  • Drying: upright on a rack or in a drying oven at 60–80 °C.
  • Check: wetting with distilled water, inspection against the light, smell check.

Run through this list every time you take a new batch of glassware or change the type of analysis: the habit of checking 4 points takes less than a minute, while a ruined analysis costs hours of work.

Frequently asked questions

In what water and in what order should labware be rinsed after washing?

Start with tap water, then distilled, and deionized if needed. Each subsequent stage should wash away the residue of the previous one, so change the water more often than seems necessary. Washing labware is built on exactly this sequence.

Can volumetric ware be washed with soap and hot water?

Not with soap: a surfactant film remains and the water no longer drains in an even sheet. Hot water isn't acceptable for all types of volumetric ware—go by the manufacturer's spec sheet and temperature range. For how to wash volumetric ware in unclear cases, check with the supplier.

How do you clean stubborn contamination—organics, resins, salt residues?

Organics and fats—surfactants or organic solvents; resins—soaking and, if necessary, chromic acid mixture under a fume hood; salts—acid. Choose the specific product and its dilution based on the type of contamination and the manufacturer's instructions. Methods for washing labware in each case rely on the chemistry of the contamination.

How do you dry labware properly to avoid streaks and dust?

Upright, neck down, in a closed cabinet or drying oven, respecting temperature limits for volumetric ware and plastic. After drying, labware for trace analysis is placed in a desiccator. Drying labware takes 4–8 hours depending on the type of oven.

How do you check that labware is really clean?

Wet the walls with distilled water: an even film with no droplets or streaks is a sign of cleanliness. Additionally, run a blank test and check the pH of the rinse with indicator paper. Checking labware cleanliness takes no more than 2–3 minutes per unit.

How do you wash labware after biological samples?

First disinfection by autoclaving at 121 °C or with a disinfectant solution, then regular washing. Protein contamination is best removed with enzymatic products at 40–50 °C right away, before it dries. After washing, vessels for culture work are sterilized again.

Can labware with cracks and chips be cleaned?

Chips on the neck and spout are dangerous for cuts and cause poor pouring, while cracks trap reagents. Such glassware isn't used for quantitative work. Minor scuffs on ground joints are acceptable only where a tight seal isn't critical.

Washing labware comes down to discipline: identify the contamination, pick a product, wash, rinse in water of decreasing hardness, dry and check. Cleaning glassware in this order extends its life and keeps analysis results reproducible.
