The Beer-Lambert law states that the absorbance of a solution is directly proportional to the concentration of the absorbing substance and the path length: A = ε·c·l. Here ε is the molar absorption coefficient in L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹, c is the concentration in mol/L, and l is the optical path length in cm. The instrument doesn't measure A directly; it measures transmittance T = I/I₀, and the two are related by A = -log T. Given ε or a single standard, concentration is a one-step calculation—below you'll find the formulas, worked numerical examples, and the reasons why the direct proportionality breaks down.

The Beer-Lambert Law in plain terms

Imagine the solution in a cuvette sliced into very thin, identical layers. Each layer absorbs the same fraction of the light that reaches it. If the first layer takes 10% of the light, the second takes 10% of the remaining 90%, the third takes 10% of 81%, and so on. The intensity doesn't fall linearly but exponentially: I = I₀·10^(-ε·c·l).

Take the logarithm of that relationship and you get a straight line: log(I₀/I) = ε·c·l. The quantity log(I₀/I) is what we call absorbance A. The logarithm is what makes the law so convenient: double the concentration and A doubles; use a cuvette twice as long and A doubles again.

The law is built from two observations. Pierre Bouguer experimentally established in 1729 how light is attenuated with layer thickness, and Johann Lambert described it mathematically in 1760. In 1852, August Beer showed that for solutions in a non-absorbing solvent, the same dependence on concentration holds. Hence the triple name—and in English-language literature, it's the Beer-Lambert law.

Beer's key assumption: a molecule absorbs light the same way regardless of how many identical molecules are nearby. As long as that holds, the law works. When molecules start interacting, reacting, or the instrument delivers the wrong light, deviations from Beer's law appear—more on that below.

The molar absorption coefficient is the absorbance of a 1 mol/L solution in a 1 cm cuvette. Of course, no one actually measures such a solution: at A values from tens to tens of thousands, the instrument wouldn't see a single photon. It's a calculated quantity—a characteristic of the substance at a given wavelength, solvent, and temperature. The larger the ε, the more sensitive the method: for ε = 10⁴, a 10⁻⁵ mol/L solution already gives A = 0.1.

If a solution contains several absorbing substances that don't interact, their absorbances add up: A = l·(ε₁c₁ + ε₂c₂ + ...). This property underlies two-wavelength analysis of mixtures and the mandatory subtraction of the blank.

Units of the molar absorption coefficient

The units of the molar absorption coefficient follow directly from the formula: ε = A/(c·l). A is dimensionless, so ε has dimensions of 1/(mol/L · cm) = L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹. In English-language tables, the same thing is written as M⁻¹·cm⁻¹. You'll sometimes encounter other units, and you need to be able to convert them:

  • 1 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹ = 1000 cm²/mol (1 L = 1000 cm³);
  • 1 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹ = 0.1 m²/mol (SI system);
  • mass absorption coefficient a = ε/M, L·g⁻¹·cm⁻¹, if concentration is in g/L;
  • specific absorbance A(1%, 1 cm)—the absorbance of a 1 g/100 mL solution in a 1 cm cuvette; this is how pharmacopoeial monographs express it. Relationship: ε = A(1%, 1 cm)·M/10.

Example 1. Converting specific absorbance to ε. For potassium dichromate K₂Cr₂O₇ in dilute sulfuric acid at 257 nm, A(1%, 1 cm) = 144.5; M(K₂Cr₂O₇) = 294.18 g/mol.

Solution: ε = 144.5 · 294.18 / 10 = 4251 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹. Let's verify with a 60 mg/L solution. That's 0.006 g in 100 mL, i.e., 0.006%: A = 144.5 · 0.006 = 0.867. Same via ε: c = 0.060/294.18 = 2.04·10⁻⁴ mol/L, A = 4251 · 2.04·10⁻⁴ · 1 = 0.867. The answers match—units converted correctly. Such dichromate solutions are exactly what's used to verify the photometric accuracy scale of spectrophotometers.

Relationship between transmittance T and absorbance A

The relationship between transmittance T and absorbance A is logarithmic:

  • A = -log T = log(1/T) = 2 - log T%;

A common mistake is assuming that at T = 25% the absorbance equals 0.75 or 75. Transmittance and the absorbed fraction are linearly related (75% absorbed), but absorbance is logarithmic.

How to calculate concentration from a known ε

The Beer-Lambert law is most often used precisely to calculate concentration. If the molar absorption coefficient is known from a reference book or a procedure, no calibration curve is needed. Here's the procedure:

  1. Select the wavelength from the procedure (usually λmax) and set it on the spectrophotometer.
  2. Fill the cuvette with the blank—solvent or a reagent blank containing everything except the analyte—and zero the instrument (A = 0, T = 100%).
  3. Rinse the cuvette with the sample solution 2–3 times, fill it, and measure A.
  4. Make sure A falls within 0.1–1.0; if not, dilute the sample or change the cuvette.
  5. Calculate c = A/(ε·l) and account for dilution.

Example 3. NADH concentration. In enzymatic assays, NADH is measured at 340 nm with ε = 6220 L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹. The reading is A = 0.311 in a 1 cm cuvette.

Solution: c = 0.311 / (6220 · 1) = 5.00·10⁻⁵ mol/L = 50.0 µmol/L. If you need mass concentration, multiply by molar mass: for NADH M = 665.4 g/mol, 5.00·10⁻⁵ · 665.4 = 0.0333 g/L = 33.3 mg/L.

Example 4. Too dilute a solution and a longer cuvette. The iron(II) complex with o-phenanthroline (ferroin) absorbs at 510 nm with ε ≈ 1.11·10⁴ L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹. In a 1 cm cuvette, the sample gave A = 0.12—at the lower edge of the working range. Transfer the same sample to a 5 cm cuvette and get A = 0.60.

Solution: c = 0.60 / (11100 · 5) = 1.08·10⁻⁵ mol/L. Check with the first measurement: 0.12 / (11100 · 1) = 1.08·10⁻⁵ mol/L—matches, as the law requires. Converted to iron: 1.08·10⁻⁵ · 55.845 · 1000 = 0.60 mg/L. That's why for faintly colored solutions you keep a set of cuvettes of different lengths—for example, a 50 mm path length glass cuvette for the visible range.

Why it's best to measure at A between 0.2 and 0.8

The instrument's transmittance error is roughly constant, say ΔT = 0.5%. But the concentration error depends on where you are on the scale: Δc/c = 0.434·ΔT/(T·A).

Calculation from a single standard

If ε is unknown or depends on reaction conditions (color development with reagents, pH, time), you determine it yourself from a solution of precisely known concentration—and the Beer-Lambert law lets you get by with just one such solution. When the standard and sample are measured in the same cuvette at the same wavelength and under identical conditions, ε·l cancels out:

c(x) = c(std) · A(x) / A(std)

The single-standard method is acceptable if linearity has already been verified, the line passes through zero, and the absorbances of sample and standard are close (preferably within ±30%). If you need to prove linearity over a series of solutions, that's a job for a calibration curve.

Example 5. Finding ε from a standard. A 2.00 mg/L iron standard with o-phenanthroline gave A = 0.398 in a 1 cm cuvette at 510 nm.

Solution: c = 2.00·10⁻³ / 55.845 = 3.58·10⁻⁵ mol/L. ε = A/(c·l) = 0.398 / (3.58·10⁻⁵ · 1) = 1.11·10⁴ L·mol⁻¹·cm⁻¹. The answer agrees with the tabulated value for ferroin—so the reagents and instrument are in order.

Example 6. Sample with dilution. A 25.0 mL sample was taken, reagents added, and the volume made up to 50.0 mL in a volumetric flask. The colored solution gave A = 0.300; the standard from Example 5 gave A = 0.398.

Solution: in the flask c = 2.00 · 0.300 / 0.398 = 1.51 mg/L. Dilution factor 50.0/25.0 = 2, so in the original sample c = 1.51 · 2 = 3.02 mg/L iron.

The accuracy of such a calculation hinges on the standard: its concentration must be known better than you want to know the result. That's why standards are prepared from certified reference materials and reagents, and dilutions are made with Class A volumetric flasks of the appropriate volume.

Deviations from Beer's law and their physical causes

Deviations from Beer's law show up as a bend in the A vs. c plot: more often downward (negative deviation), less often upward. The Beer-Lambert law holds strictly only for dilute, clear solutions and monochromatic light, so the causes fall into three groups.

True (physical) deviations

  • High concentration. Above roughly 0.01 mol/L, molecules are so close that they affect each other's charge distribution, and the ability to absorb light changes. The law in its strict form is a law for dilute solutions.
  • Change in refractive index. In concentrated solutions, the refractive index increases with concentration, and ε depends on it.

Chemical deviations

  • Equilibria in solution. When dichromate is diluted in neutral water, some Cr₂O₇²⁻ converts to HCrO₄⁻ and CrO₄²⁻ with a different spectrum, so A falls non-proportionally. Fixed acidity helps (dichromate is measured in dilute H₂SO₄).
  • Association and dimerization. Dyes like methylene blue form dimers as concentration increases, and dimers have a different absorption band.
  • pH and incomplete complexation. Indicators and colored complexes require strictly controlled pH and excess reagent; otherwise part of the substance remains in an uncolored form.

Instrumental deviations

  • Non-monochromatic light. The law is derived for a single wavelength. If the spectral bandwidth is comparable to the width of the substance's band, the beam contains wavelengths with different ε, and the plot bends downward. The slit width should be noticeably smaller than the absorption band width—usually no more than a tenth of it.
  • Stray light. A small fraction s of light bypasses the monochromator and reaches the detector. The measured A = -log((T + s)/(1 + s)), and at high A the error is huge.
  • Turbidity, bubbles, fluorescence. Scattering by particles inflates A; sample fluorescence deflates it.

Common student mistakes and what you'll need for measurements

  • Confusing transmittance and absorbance. Plugging T% into A = εcl instead of A. First convert: A = 2 - log T%, then calculate.
  • Forgetting the units of l. A 10 mm cuvette means l = 1 cm; a 20 mm cuvette means l = 2 cm. If you plug in 10, your concentration will come out 10 times too low.
  • Using ε for a different wavelength or solvent. Ferroin's ε at 510 nm differs from that at 480 nm; the tabulated value refers to the conditions in the procedure.
  • Ignoring dilution. Calculating the concentration in the volumetric flask and reporting it as the sample concentration.
  • Zeroing the instrument with water when the reagents are colored. The blank must contain everything except the analyte.
  • Measuring at A above 1.5–2 instead of diluting. The error grows severalfold, and the bend in the plot gets mistaken for a property of the substance.
  • Placing the cuvette in the beam with a dirty face. Fingerprints and droplets on the optical faces add hundredths to A—comparable to the signal from a dilute solution.

For both teaching and routine photometry, you'll need a spectrophotometer or a colorimeter covering the required wavelength range, plus a set of cuvettes. Glass won't work for UV measurements (proteins at 280 nm, nucleic acids at 260 nm, dichromate at 257 nm), since it becomes opaque below roughly 320 nm. That's why you need quartz cuvettes with a 20 mm path length or 10 mm. Cuvettes come as matched pairs: one for the reference solution and one for the sample. Check that, when filled with the same solution, they give an absorbance difference of no more than a few thousandths. You'll also need Class A volumetric flasks and pipettes, standard solutions, and an analytical balance for weighing.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between optical density, absorbance, and extinction?

They all refer to the same quantity, A = lg(I₀/I). Optical density is the standard term in Russian-language methods and pharmacopoeias; absorbance (absorbancy) comes from the English word; extinction is an older name. What older books call the molar extinction coefficient is now known as the molar absorption coefficient.

Can the Beer–Lambert law be applied to turbid solutions?

No. In a turbid solution, light is not only absorbed but also scattered, and the instrument reads that scattering as absorbance. Filter or centrifuge the sample; if turbidity itself is what you're measuring, use turbidimetry with its own calibration curve.

Why does the formula use a base-10 logarithm when physics uses e?

Physicists write the light attenuation law using the natural logarithm: I = I₀·e^(-kl). Chemists and instrument manufacturers use base 10, and all reference ε values are given on that basis. Converting is simple: natural optical density is 2.303 times greater than base-10 optical density.

What should I do if the optical density of the solution is greater than 1.5?

Dilute the sample by a precisely known factor (for example, 5.00 mL into a 25.0 mL flask, a factor of 5), or use a cuvette with a shorter path length, such as 0.5 or 0.2 cm. Then recalculate the result to account for the dilution or the new l. Don't measure it as is — stray light and growing error will distort the reading.

To handle Beer–Lambert law problems with confidence, keep three things in mind: A = -lg T, the formula A = εcl with l in centimeters and c in mol/L, and a working range of A around 0.2–0.8. If your result doesn't match expectations, first check the reference solution, the cuvette path length, and the dilution, then look for chemical and instrumental causes of the discrepancy.
