To figure out how to dilute a solution to the target concentration, one formula is enough: C1·V1 = C2·V2. The amount of solute doesn't change when you dilute, so you calculate the volume of stock solution as V1 = C2·V2 / C1, measure it with a volumetric pipette into a volumetric flask of volume V2, and bring it to the mark with solvent. For example, to make 100 mL of a 0.1 M solution from a 1 M solution, take 10 mL of stock with a 10 mL pipette and bring it to the mark with water in a 100 mL flask.

The dilution formula and what each letter means

The dilution formula comes from a simple fact: when you add solvent, the amount of dissolved substance doesn't increase or decrease. Whatever was in the aliquot you took is what you'll have in the final solution.

C1·V1 = C2·V2, where:

  • C1 - concentration of the stock solution;
  • V1 - volume of stock solution to measure out;
  • C2 - desired concentration of the final solution;
  • V2 - final volume of the solution (the volume of the volumetric flask), not the volume of water added.

In English-language textbooks it's written as C1V1 = C2V2, which gives the working rearrangements: V1 = C2·V2 / C1 (how much to take), C2 = C1·V1 / V2 (what you'll get), V2 = C1·V1 / C2 (what volume to make it up to).

The units can be anything, but they must match on both sides: mol/L and mol/L, mg/L and mg/L, mL and mL. If C1 is in g/L and C2 is in mg/L, convert to the same units first (1 g/L = 1000 mg/L). For solutions in volume percent (% v/v) and mass-volume percent (% w/v, g/100 mL), the formula works directly too. For mass fraction (% w/w), it's better to calculate using masses: m1·w1 = m2·w2, because the density of the solution changes on dilution. If the starting concentration is known only as mass percent, you first need to convert to mol/L using density - that's a separate problem.

Dilution factor and the "1:10" notation

The dilution factor F shows how many times the concentration has dropped: F = C1 / C2 = V2 / V1. If you made 100 mL from 10 mL, F = 10. In biology and clinical labs it's often written as a power: a 10⁻³ dilution means F = 1000.

There's a trap with the "1:10" notation. In analytical chemistry and microbiology, it usually means "1 part sample in 10 parts total volume": 1 mL + 9 mL solvent, F = 10. But in some protocols and on concentrate labels, "1:10" means "1 part concentrate to 10 parts water", i.e., 1 + 10 and F = 11. The resulting concentration is about 9% lower. Before you start, check how the method defines the dilution, and write it unambiguously in your log: "1 mL in 10 mL" or "F = 10".

How to dilute a solution to the target concentration - step by step

  1. Write down C1, C2, and the desired V2 in the same units. Calculate the dilution factor F = C1 / C2.
  2. Choose a volumetric flask of volume V2 or the nearest larger standard size (25, 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000 mL) if you need extra solution.
  3. Calculate V1 = V2 / F. If V1 doesn't match any pipette volume, change V2 (use a different flask) or dilute in two steps.
  4. Add about one-third to one-half of the solvent to the flask. For acids this is essential: pour acid into water, not water into acid.
  5. Rinse the pipette with a small portion of stock solution, measure V1, and transfer to the flask, holding the tip against the wall. Let the solution drain and wait for the drainage time specified on the pipette.
  6. Mix with swirling; if the solution heats up or cools down (acids, ethanol), wait until it reaches room temperature.
  7. Bring to the mark with solvent, reading the bottom of the meniscus; add the last drops with a pipette or wash bottle.
  8. Stopper and mix by inverting 10-15 times. Label: substance, concentration, date, who prepared it.

Worked examples with numbers

Four typical problems show how to dilute a solution to the target concentration in different units: mol/L, volume percent, and g/L.

Example 1. Acid solution from a more concentrated one

Given. You have 1.0 M HCl. You need 250 mL of 0.1 M HCl.

Solution. F = 1.0 / 0.1 = 10. V1 = C2·V2 / C1 = 0.1 mol/L · 250 mL / 1.0 mol/L = 25 mL.

Answer. Use a 25 mL pipette to transfer 25 mL of 1.0 M HCl into a 250 mL volumetric flask containing about 100 mL of water, then bring to the mark.

Example 2. 70% ethanol from 96%

Given. You need 100 mL of 70% (v/v) ethanol from 96% (v/v).

Solution. The dilution factor here is small: F = 96 / 70 ≈ 1.37. V1 = 70 · 100 / 96 = 72.9 mL.

Answer. Measure 72.9 mL of 96% ethanol (with a burette or graduated cylinder - for disinfection that's accurate enough) and bring to 100 mL with water in a volumetric flask or cylinder. It's wrong to mix "72.9 mL ethanol + 27.1 mL water": when ethanol and water mix, the volume contracts, so you'll get less than 100 mL and the concentration will be slightly above 70%. Always make up to the final volume rather than adding volumes.

Example 3. How much water to add to the remainder of a solution

Given. You have 20 mL of 0.5 M NaCl left. You need to dilute it to 0.2 M.

Solution. V2 = C1·V1 / C2 = 0.5 · 20 / 0.2 = 50 mL. You need to add about 50 - 20 = 30 mL of water.

Answer. For dilute aqueous salt solutions, volumes add up almost exactly, so 30 mL of water will give 0.2 M. For precise work, it's better to transfer 20 mL into a 50 mL flask and bring to the mark.

Example 4. What concentration you'll get

Given. 5 mL of CuSO₄ solution at 2.0 g/L was brought to 50 mL with water.

Solution. C2 = C1·V1 / V2 = 2.0 · 5 / 50 = 0.2 g/L, F = 10.

Answer. 0.2 g/L, or 200 mg/L.

1:10 dilution and other ratios - which pipette and which flask

The most common question is how to do a 1:10 dilution without calculations and with minimal error. There are three options: 10 mL into a 100 mL flask (analytical work, standards), 1 mL + 9 mL in a tube (microbiology, series), or 100 µL + 900 µL in a microtube (biochemistry, PCR, ELISA). The first option is the most accurate because both the pipette and the flask operate at their nominal volume.

General rule for choosing: the larger V1, the smaller the relative error. A Class A volumetric pipette of 1 mL has a tolerance of about ±0.008 mL, which is 0.8% of volume, while a 10 mL pipette has a tolerance of ±0.02 mL - only 0.2%. A Class A 100 mL flask gives ±0.1 mL, i.e., 0.1%. Total for the "10 mL / 100 mL" pair is about 0.22%, for "1 mL / 100 mL" about 0.8%. That's why for standards and working solutions in analytical work you use Class A volumetric glassware, and leave Class B for auxiliary solutions.

Serial dilution - how to calculate the concentration in each tube

Serial dilution is a chain of dilutions where each subsequent tube is prepared from the previous one with the same factor. The concentration after n steps is: Cn = C0 / Fⁿ, i.e., the same dilution formula applies to each step, and the overall dilution factor is the product of the factors of all steps. With F = 10, the concentrations form the series 1; 0.1; 0.01; 0.001. That's why a tenfold series is called logarithmic. There are also semi-logarithmic series with a step of √10 ≈ 3.16 and twofold series: 100; 50; 25; 12.5; 6.25 - these are usually used for antibody titers and calibration curves.

Example 5. Tenfold series of a microbial suspension

Given. The original suspension contains approximately 10⁸ CFU/mL. You need to obtain dilutions 10⁻¹...10⁻⁶ and plate them.

Solution. Add 9 mL of sterile saline to six tubes. Add 1 mL of suspension to the first, mix, use a new sterile pipette to transfer 1 mL to the second, and so on. Concentrations: 10⁷, 10⁶, 10⁵, 10⁴, 10³, 10² CFU/mL. Plate 0.1 mL from the last tubes.

Calculating the result: 56 colonies grew on the plate from the 10⁻⁶ dilution when 0.1 mL was plated. N = 56 · 10⁶ / 0.1 = 5.6·10⁸ CFU/mL in the original sample.

Answer. The original suspension contains 5.6·10⁸ CFU/mL. Note that the plating volume also enters the calculation as a factor of 1 / 0.1 = 10.

Why error accumulates in a series

The error of each step carries over to the next. If one step gives 0.5% random error, after three steps you get about 0.5·√3 ≈ 0.87%, and systematic error (e.g., a dispenser that consistently delivers 1% less) simply adds up: three steps - about 3%. That's why for calibration solutions in analytical work the chain is kept short, while in microbiology you change the tip or pipette at each step and mix thoroughly: carrying over a drop from the outside of the tip is a common cause of falsely high results in the last tubes.

How to prepare a working solution from a stock standard

In AAS, ICP, and photometry labs, the question "how to prepare a working solution from a stock" comes up every day. Ready-made stock standards usually have a concentration of 1000 mg/L, while calibrations are built in the range 0.5-10 mg/L or lower. A direct calculation for 2 mg/L in a 100 mL flask gives V1 = 2 · 100 / 1000 = 0.2 mL - too little for a glass pipette.

A convenient scheme is via an intermediate solution. First, 10 mL of the 1000 mg/L standard is brought to 100 mL: that gives 100 mg/L (F = 10). Then each calibration point is prepared separately from the intermediate solution, not from each other:

Stock and certified standards are easy to find in the reagents and standards section, and the intermediate solution, if the method allows, should be stored separately and labeled with the preparation date. The lower the concentration, the shorter the solution's shelf life: metals at the µg/L level adsorb on the walls, so such working solutions are usually prepared on the day of analysis and acidified as the method requires.

Watch the matrix too. If the standard is dissolved in 2-5% HNO₃ and the samples are in 1% HNO₃, bring the working solutions to the mark with the same acid solution as the samples, not with pure water.

What glassware and equipment you'll need

  • Class A volumetric pipettes of 1, 2, 5, 10, 25 mL - the main tool for accurate V1. For the most common factor of 10, a 10 mL Class A borosilicate glass pipette is convenient.
  • Class A volumetric flasks of 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000 mL. For the "10 mL in 100 mL" pair, a 100 mL Class A volumetric flask with a rim will work. For light-sensitive solutions (silver, iodine, some dyes), use amber flasks; they're also available among laboratory glassware.
  • Mechanical dispenser for volumes under 1 mL and when serial dilutions are done in microtubes, e.g., a single-channel Proline Plus dispenser 100-1000 µL. Work in the upper part of the range: the dispenser's tolerance is specified in microliters for the nominal volume, so 100 µL on a 100-1000 µL dispenser has a noticeably larger relative error than 100 µL on a 20-200 µL dispenser.
  • Bulb or pipette controller (never pipette by mouth), wash bottle, beakers for aliquot sampling, tubes with a rack for series, labels or marker.

Common mistakes when diluting

  • Confusing V2 with the volume of water. For 100 mL of solution with F = 10, you need not "10 mL + 100 mL water" but 10 mL brought to 100 mL. The "10 + 100" mistake gives F = 11 and a 9% underestimate.
  • Different units in the formula. C1 in g/L, C2 in mg/L - and the answer is off by a factor of 1000. Convert everything to the same units before substituting.
  • Ambiguous "1:10" notation. Decide whether it's 1 + 9 or 1 + 10, and write F or "1 mL in 10 mL" in the protocol.
  • Measuring with a cylinder where a pipette is needed. A cylinder's error is several times larger than that of a Class A volumetric pipette. For standards, titrants, and calibration solutions, a cylinder won't do.
  • Too small V1. Measuring 0.2 mL with glass or 50 µL with a 1000 µL dispenser. Better to use two dilution steps.
  • Bringing a warm solution to the mark. After diluting acid or ethanol, the solution heats up or contracts; if you bring it to the mark immediately, the level will be below the mark after cooling.
  • Poor mixing. Invert the stoppered flask 10-15 times, otherwise a more concentrated layer remains at the bottom and an aliquot from the top will be weaker.
  • Not rinsing the pipette with stock solution. Water droplets on the walls dilute the aliquot before it's even transferred.
  • Using one tip for all steps in a serial dilution. Carryover from the outside surface inflates the concentration in the last tubes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is C1V1 C2V2 suitable for percentage solutions?

For % v/v and g/100 mL — yes. For mass fraction, the solution dilution formula is written using masses. For example, to make a 10% solution from 200 g of a 30% solution: m2 = 200 · 30 / 10 = 600 g, so you add 600 - 200 = 400 g of water to the solution, weighing it on a scale.

Can I dilute directly 1000-fold?

You can, if you have a 1 mL pipette and a 1000 mL flask. But the error of such a single step is about 0.8%, while two steps of 10- and 100-fold with a 10 mL pipette give about 0.3%. For standards, two steps are more advantageous.

How does the dilution factor differ from the dilution ratio?

They're the same thing, just written differently. In microbiology, a dilution factor F = 1000 is written as a 10⁻³ dilution or 1:1000. To get back to the original concentration, multiply the result by F.

How do I record a serial dilution in the lab notebook?

You note the original sample, the step (e.g., 1 mL into 10 mL, F = 10), the number of steps, and the solvent. For each tube, write the final dilution: 10⁻¹, 10⁻², 10⁻³. That way, when you recalculate the result, you won't have to remember which tube you plated from.

What should I do if the level drops below the mark after dilution?

If the solution has simply cooled, top it up to the mark with solvent and mix again. But if you've overfilled it above the mark, the solution must be prepared anew: you can't just remove the excess, the concentration has already changed.

If you're again wondering how to dilute a solution to the desired concentration, remember the key point: V1 = C2·V2 / C1, V2 is the volume of the flask, not of the water, and the larger the volume you pipette, the more accurate the result. Before your next series of dilutions, write out the required fold-dilutions and pick matching 'pipette + flask' pairs from the table above — that way the calculation comes down to choosing the glassware.
