Vacuum filtration is a way of separating a solid from a liquid by creating a vacuum below the filter so that atmospheric pressure forces the solution through the paper or porous plate dozens of times faster than gravity alone. The classic setup consists of a Büchner funnel, a Büchner flask with a side arm, a safety trap flask, and a vacuum source (a water aspirator or an electric pump). The golden rule of order: wet the filter and turn on the vacuum before you pour the suspension, and at the end always release the vacuum first and only then turn off the pump.

What goes into a vacuum filtration setup

The layout is always the same; only the sizes change. From top to bottom, following the path of liquid and air:

  • Büchner funnel – porcelain with a perforated bottom on which filter paper is placed, or glass with a sintered porous plate (a fritted filter).
  • Seal – a rubber stopper with a hole or a conical sleeve that holds the funnel in the neck of the flask without a gap.
  • Büchner flask – a thick-walled conical flask with a side arm; the filtrate collects in it.
  • Trap – a second thick-walled flask or a bottle with a stopcock placed between the flask and the pump.
  • Vacuum hose – thick-walled so it doesn't collapse under vacuum.
  • Vacuum source – a water aspirator on a tap, a diaphragm pump, or another electric pump.

Always secure the flask and the trap with a clamp on a stand at the neck: under vacuum, the funnel with precipitate makes the flask top-heavy and unstable, and the hose can easily tip it over. The hose must fit tightly onto the flask side arm and the pump inlet.

What a Büchner flask is for and what volume to choose

A Büchner flask looks like an ordinary conical flask, but there are two differences. First, the thick walls: at a residual pressure of 10–20 kPa, almost one kilogram-force presses on every square centimetre of wall, and a thin-walled Erlenmeyer flask can implode, sending glass flying. Second, the side arm for the hose, which lets you evacuate air without obstructing the funnel on top. In catalogues it is also called a Büchner filtration flask for vacuum filtration.

Choose the volume based on the expected amount of filtrate plus the wash portions. The liquid level must not reach the side arm, or the filtrate will be sucked into the hose and trap. A practical rule: use a flask about twice the total liquid volume.

Example. You filter 150 mL of suspension and wash the precipitate with 3 portions of 20 mL. Filtrate: 150 + 3·20 = 210 mL. A 250 mL flask would be filled almost to the side arm – you need a 500 mL flask. For teaching syntheses with 50–100 mL of suspension, a 250 mL Büchner flask with a 29/32 joint is sufficient.

When vacuum filtering hot solutions, remember that you must not heat a Büchner flask on a hotplate or burner – thick glass does not tolerate temperature changes well. You can filter a hot solution into it, but first warm the flask with hot solvent so the glass heats up gradually.

The trap between pump and flask and the vacuum source

The trap between pump and flask protects against two problems. With a water aspirator, if the water pressure drops or is switched off, tap water is sucked back into the line and ends up in the filtrate – this is back-siphoning. With an electric pump, the trap keeps solvent vapours and filtrate droplets out of the pump if the flask overfills. A trap with a stopcock or a clamp on the side arm is most convenient: open it to let air in and release the vacuum in one motion.

How to use a Büchner funnel step by step

This is the procedure for vacuum filtration in organic lab courses, recrystallization, and precipitate isolation in analysis.

  1. Clamp the Büchner flask at the neck, insert the stopper or sleeve, and place the funnel on top. Run the hose from the flask side arm to the trap, and from the trap to the pump. Open the trap stopcock.
  2. Place the filter paper on the bottom of the funnel. It must lie flat and cover all holes but not extend onto the walls.
  3. Wet the filter with the same solvent used in the suspension (a few millilitres).
  4. Turn on the pump and close the trap stopcock. The filter will be pulled down, and the funnel will seat tightly. If you hear hissing at the stopper, adjust the funnel.
  5. Swirl the suspension and pour it into the centre of the filter in portions, never letting the liquid level drop below the solid, until you have transferred the bulk.
  6. Rinse the remaining precipitate from the beaker or flask with small portions of filtrate or cold solvent and transfer to the filter as well.
  7. Wash the precipitate with cold solvent: washing the precipitate on the filter is described in a separate section below.
  8. Leave the precipitate under vacuum for 2–5 minutes to suck out the solvent. You can press the cake flat with a stopper or spatula to close cracks.
  9. Open the trap stopcock or disconnect the hose from the flask side arm – the vacuum is released. Only then turn off the pump or shut off the water.
  10. Remove the funnel, invert it over filter paper or a watch glass, and take out the cake together with the filter.

Step 9 is the most important, and it is the key thing to know about using a Büchner funnel. If you turn off a water aspirator while the system is under vacuum, tap water will flow into the trap, or without a trap directly into the filtrate. If you turn off an oil pump under vacuum, oil can be drawn into the line.

How to choose a filter for a Büchner funnel

Choose the filter based on two parameters: diameter and paper type.

Diameter. Measure the inner diameter of the perforated bottom. You need the largest standard circle of filter paper (42.5, 55, 70, 90, 110, 125, 150 mm) that lies flat and covers all holes. A filter folded up the walls creates creases under which precipitate can sneak. A filter smaller than the bottom leaves holes open at the edge – cloudy filtrate is guaranteed. If the right size is not available, carefully trim a circle with scissors to fit the funnel bottom.

Paper type. For coarse crystalline precipitates, use fast paper; for fine precipitates, medium; for thin amorphous precipitates and barium sulfate, dense slow paper. Sometimes two layers of paper are used: the bottom one protects the top from being forced into the holes.

Example of cake thickness calculation. You expect 12 g of crystalline precipitate (dry basis) with a bulk density of about 0.6 g/cm³. Cake volume: V = m / ρ = 12 / 0.6 = 20 cm³. Filter area S = π·d² / 4:

  • d = 55 mm: S = 23.8 cm², thickness h = V / S = 20 / 23.8 = 0.84 cm;
  • d = 70 mm: S = 38.5 cm², h = 0.52 cm;
  • d = 90 mm: S = 63.6 cm², h = 0.31 cm.

A layer of 3–6 mm is most convenient: it washes evenly and dries quickly; a layer about a centimetre or thicker filters and washes slowly, while too thin a layer cracks. Here, 70 and 90 mm circles are suitable; with 55 mm the layer would be almost a centimetre thick. Check which circle fits a particular funnel, for example a 10 cm porcelain Büchner funnel, by its bottom.

A glass filter is convenient when the precipitate must be weighed: dry the filter to constant weight beforehand, then after filtration dry it with the precipitate and find the precipitate mass by difference. The principle is the same as preparing crucibles – bringing to constant weight with cooling in a desiccator between weighings.

Glass filter porosity G1–G4 and pore-size classes

Glass filter porosity G1–G4 is an old designation still printed on funnels and in catalogues. The modern one denotes the class by maximum pore size in micrometres: P160, P100, P40, P16. In Russian marking you may see POR 160, POR 100, POR 40, POR 16.

Choose glass filter porosity G1–G4 according to the precipitate. Rule: the maximum pore size should be slightly smaller than the smallest particles of the precipitate. Using a finer filter 'just in case' is not beneficial: G4 for large crystals filters many times slower and clogs faster. The porous plate is rated for a pressure differential of no more than 1 bar: normal vacuum stays within this limit, but do not exceed it when pressure filtering from above. Before first use, wash a new filter with solvent under vacuum, and clean it immediately after work – dried precipitate in the pores is much harder to remove. Clean the pores with whatever dissolves the precipitate: inorganic salts and oxides with hydrochloric acid, organics with a suitable solvent, and then follow the same procedure as for washing laboratory glassware: water, then distilled water, and drying without sudden heating.

Washing the precipitate on the filter without losses

Washing the precipitate on the filter is needed to remove the mother liquor with impurities that remains between the crystals. The main rule: many small portions are better than one large one.

If the cake retains a volume Vr of mother liquor, and the precipitate is washed with n portions of volume Vw with mixing, the fraction of impurity remaining after washing is:

x = (Vr / (Vr + Vw))ⁿ

where Vr and Vw are in millilitres, and x is the fraction of the initial amount of impurity.

Example 1. The cake retains 5 mL of mother liquor, and you have 30 mL of solvent for washing.

  • one portion of 30 mL: x = 5 / 35 = 0.143, 14.3 % impurity remains;
  • three portions of 10 mL: x = (5 / 15)³ = 0.037, 3.7 % remains;
  • six portions of 5 mL: x = (5 / 10)⁶ = 0.0156, 1.6 % remains.

Same solvent, but 9 times less impurity – that is how washing the precipitate on the filter with small portions works. The formula is idealized; in practice the gain is slightly less, but the order of magnitude holds.

Example 2 – product losses. The product solubility in cold solvent is 0.5 g per 100 mL, and you wash with 3 portions of 10 mL. Upper estimate of losses if each portion becomes saturated: 0.5 · 30 / 100 = 0.15 g. With a yield of 5 g, that is 3 %. Therefore, wash with ice-cold solvent in which the product is less soluble, and do not leave liquid on the precipitate longer than necessary.

Washing procedure:

  1. Open the trap stopcock (release the vacuum) so the liquid does not rush through cracks.
  2. Pour a portion of cold solvent so that it covers the entire precipitate.
  3. Gently stir the top layer with a spatula without touching the paper.
  4. Close the stopcock and suck the liquid dry.
  5. Repeat 2–4 times, then dry the precipitate under vacuum.

Why vacuum filtration is faster and when the filtrate boils

The flow rate of a liquid through a filter is proportional to the pressure differential. Under gravity, this is created only by the liquid column: ΔP = ρ·g·h.

Example 3. Let's compare gravity filtration with vacuum filtration. An aqueous solution column above the filter h = 5 cm: ΔP = 1000 · 9.81 · 0.05 = 490 Pa. Under vacuum with a residual pressure of 20 kPa: ΔP = 101.3 - 20 = 81.3 kPa = 81,300 Pa. The ratio 81,300 / 490 ≈ 166 — the driving force is more than 160 times greater.

The same pressure difference presses the filter against the bottom: F = ΔP·S. For a 90 mm circle (S = 63.6 cm² = 0.00636 m²): F = 81,300 · 0.00636 ≈ 517 N, which is about 53 kgf. That's why a loosely placed or unwetted filter tears at the edge, and dry paper on fine perforations gets pushed through.

Example 4 — boiling of the filtrate. A liquid boils when the pressure above it drops to its vapor pressure. Compare the residual pressure in the flask with the vapor pressure of the solvent at the filtrate's temperature.

Hot water boils too: at 60 °C its vapor pressure is about 19.9 kPa, so a hot aqueous filtrate at a residual 10–20 kPa boils, sprays droplets into the outlet, and cools, while the dissolved substance crystallizes right in the filter pores. To keep vacuum filtration from turning into evaporation, reduce the vacuum (slightly open the trap valve) and don't leave a volatile filtrate under vacuum any longer than necessary.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

  • Turning off the pump before releasing the vacuum. Water from the water-jet pump gets sucked into the trap or into the filtrate. Always open the trap valve or disconnect the hose first, then turn off the pump.
  • No trap. The trap between the pump and the flask is just a simple bottle, and without it an overfilled flask sends filtrate into the pump, while back-suction sends water into the product.
  • Dry or loose filter. The suspension slips under the edge of the paper and the filtrate turns cloudy. Wet the filter and turn on the vacuum before pouring the suspension.
  • Filter larger than the bottom. A common mistake for those who don't know how to match a filter to a Büchner funnel: the edges fold up against the walls and the precipitate slips under the creases.
  • Thin-walled flask instead of a Büchner flask. Risk of implosion. Before use, inspect the flask for cracks and chips.
  • Filtrate above the outlet. Stop the filtration, pour off the filtrate and continue.
  • Cracks in the filter cake. Air passes through the cracks, the solvent isn't sucked out, and washing bypasses the precipitate. Press down the cake and smooth out the cracks.
  • Washing with warm solvent or in one big portion. More losses and worse purification.
  • Dense glass filter for a coarse precipitate. Filtration slows down several-fold and the pores clog.

Büchner funnels and side-arm flasks in various sizes are available in the laboratory glassware section — match the funnel diameter to the flask neck and the filter size.

Frequently asked questions

What makes vacuum filtration better than ordinary filtration through a fluted filter?

It's faster, gives a drier precipitate, and lets you wash it in place. Ordinary filtration through a fluted filter is chosen when you need the filtrate and the precipitate is waste — for example, hot filtration to remove impurities before crystallization, so the solution doesn't cool and doesn't boil under vacuum.

Can I use a second Büchner flask instead of a trap?

Yes, if it's thick-walled, clamped securely, and closed with a stopper holding a glass tube. The hose from the first flask connects to the tube in the stopper, and the side arm of the second flask connects to the pump.

Can I filter a hot solution under vacuum?

Yes, if the solvent doesn't boil at that vacuum level. Pre-warm the flask and funnel with hot solvent, and keep the vacuum moderate. For volatile solvents, ordinary hot filtration is better.

How can I tell the filter is the wrong one?

A cloudy filtrate in the first few seconds signals a gap at the edge or pores that are too coarse. If the liquid barely passes through despite good vacuum, the filter is too dense or clogged with fine precipitate.

How does a Hirsch funnel differ from a Büchner funnel?

A Hirsch funnel is conical with a small bottom. It's used for small amounts of precipitate (tens to hundreds of milligrams) so the layer doesn't get spread as a thin film over a large area.

To make vacuum filtration fast and loss-free, remember three things. The Büchner flask is clamped securely and always runs through a trap. Turn on the vacuum before pouring the suspension, and release it before turning off the pump. Wash the precipitate with several small portions of cold solvent. Match the filter to the funnel bottom, and choose the porosity of a glass filter (G1–G4) by the size of the finest precipitate particles.
