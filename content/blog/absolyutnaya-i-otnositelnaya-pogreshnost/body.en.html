Absolute and relative error tell you how much you can trust a measured number. Absolute error Δx is the possible deviation of a result from the true value, expressed in the same units as the quantity itself (mL, g, mol/L). Relative error δ = Δx / x · 100% is the same uncertainty expressed as a fraction or percentage of the result. For a lab report, you only need three steps: estimate Δ for each measurement from the tolerances of your glassware and balance, combine the relative errors using the formula for the derived quantity, and round the answer so that the last digit of the result is in the same decimal place as the uncertainty.

Absolute and Relative Error: Definitions and Formulas

Let x be the measured value and x₀ the true or accepted (reference, certificate) value.

  • Absolute error: Δx = |x - x₀|. It has the same units as x. If the true value is unknown, Δx is estimated from the instrument and the scatter of repeated measurements.
  • Relative error: δx = Δx / x · 100% (when the true value is known, divide by x₀; for small errors the difference is negligible). It is dimensionless and convenient for comparing different measurements.

Example 1. A student determined the concentration of an HCl solution to be 0.1012 mol/L, while the certificate for the standard fixanal states 0.1000 mol/L. Absolute error: Δ = 0.1012 - 0.1000 = 0.0012 mol/L. Relative error: δ = 0.0012 / 0.1000 · 100% = 1.2%. For an educational titration this is high: with careful work the error is usually 0.2–0.5%.

Example 2. Water was taken with a 10 mL pipette, weighed, and the mass converted to volume, giving 9.85 mL. Δ = 10.00 - 9.85 = 0.15 mL, δ = 0.15 / 10.00 · 100% = 1.5%. This error is several times the pipette tolerance and almost always means the pipette was not held vertically, the drainage time was not observed, or the liquid was not drawn exactly to the mark (meniscus above the mark). Blowing out the last drop "to deliver" (blow-out) instead gives a volume that is too high.

Why you need both quantities. An error of 0.1 mL when measuring 100 mL is 0.1%, but when measuring 1 mL it is already 10%. Absolute error says nothing about measurement quality until it is compared with the result itself.

Systematic and Random Error: What's the Difference

Absolute and relative error describe the size of the error, while systematic and random error describe its nature. They differ in how they behave when the experiment is repeated.

A third type is a gross error (blunder): using the wrong pipette, recording 21.45 instead of 12.45, over-titrating. Such a result is rejected, not averaged with the others.

A simple test: if five replicate titrations give 12.45, 12.50, 12.40, 12.48, and 12.42 mL, the scatter is random error. If all five agree but the concentration is still 1% higher than the certificate value, look for a systematic error: good precision of replicates does not guarantee accuracy.

Where to Get Δ for Glassware and Balances

Error in lab work almost always comes from the tolerances of volumetric glassware, weighing error, and reading error. To justify the absolute and relative error in your report, take numbers from the markings rather than guessing. The class tolerance is printed on the glassware itself (e.g., "100 ml ± 0.10 ml A"), and that is what you use as Δ. Below are typical values for class A; class B tolerances are roughly twice as large, and the differences between classes are explained in the article on class A and B volumetric glassware.

A few rules that save time when defending your report:

  • Burette. The titrant volume is the difference of two readings, so the meniscus reading error is added to the tolerance. For a 25 mL class A burette, this is about 0.01 mL per reading (one tenth of the 0.1 mL division). Total: 0.03 + 2 · 0.01 = 0.05 mL for the delivered volume.
  • Balance. A sample weighed by difference (container with substance minus empty container) involves two readings, so Δm = 2 · 0.0001 = 0.0002 g. On a Sartorius Entris II analytical balance, a 0.2500 g sample gives δm = 0.08%, while a 0.0500 g sample gives 0.4%. This is why methods require a minimum sample mass.
  • Volumetric flask. Use the tolerance from the marking; the error in filling to the mark is smaller than the tolerance when done correctly. For a 100 mL class A volumetric flask, this is ± 0.10 mL.
  • Reference values. Molar masses and constants are taken with one or two extra significant figures, so their contribution to the error is negligible.

Error of Indirect Measurements: How to Combine Errors

Concentration, mass fraction, and density in lab work are not measured directly but calculated from masses and volumes. The error of indirect measurements is found using two rules.

  1. Sum or difference (y = a + b or y = a - b): absolute errors add. Δy = Δa + Δb.
  2. Product or quotient (y = a · b / c): relative errors add. δy = δa + δb + δc. If a quantity is raised to power k, its relative error is multiplied by k.

For the derived quantity itself, absolute and relative error are simply related: Δy = y · δy / 100%.

This gives the maximum (worst-case) estimate: the worst case where all errors add in the same direction. In practice, errors are independent and partially cancel, so in analytical chemistry the root-sum-square combination is more often used:

δy = √(δa² + δb² + δc²)

This is the uncertainty propagation rule, well described in the NIST guide on combining uncertainty components. In coursework, either method is acceptable, but state which one you used in the report. The root-sum-square estimate is never larger than the maximum estimate and is noticeably smaller when two or more contributions are comparable.

A useful consequence: in a root-sum-square sum, the largest component dominates. If δV = 0.40% and δm = 0.04%, the mass contribution is almost invisible, and it is the volume measurement that needs improvement.

Worked Examples with Chemical Glassware

In each example below, absolute and relative error are calculated using the same scheme: tolerance of each measurement, relative contributions, sum, conversion back to units of the result.

Example 3: Concentration of a Standard Na₂CO₃ Solution

Problem. A sample of anhydrous Na₂CO₃, m = 0.5300 g (weighed by difference on an analytical balance), was dissolved in a 100 mL class A volumetric flask. M(Na₂CO₃) = 105.99 g/mol. Find the molar concentration and its error. This is a typical problem on the error of indirect measurements.

Solution.

  1. C = m / (M · V) = 0.5300 / (105.99 · 0.1000) = 0.050005 mol/L.
  2. Δm = 0.0002 g, δm = 0.0002 / 0.5300 · 100% = 0.038%.
  3. ΔV = 0.10 mL, δV = 0.10 / 100.00 · 100% = 0.10%.
  4. The error in M is negligible.
  5. Maximum estimate: δC = 0.038 + 0.10 = 0.14%; ΔC = 0.050005 · 0.0014 = 0.00007 mol/L.
  6. Root-sum-square estimate: δC = √(0.038² + 0.10²) = 0.11%; ΔC = 0.00005 mol/L.

Answer. C(Na₂CO₃) = 0.05000 ± 0.00007 mol/L (δ = 0.14%, maximum estimate). The main contribution comes from the flask, not the balance.

Example 4: NaOH Concentration from a Titration

Problem. A 10.00 mL aliquot of NaOH (class A pipette, ± 0.02 mL) was titrated with HCl solution of C = 0.1000 ± 0.0002 mol/L. The titration required 12.45 mL from a 25 mL burette (± 0.05 mL including two readings).

Solution.

  1. C(NaOH) = C(HCl) · V(HCl) / V(NaOH) = 0.1000 · 12.45 / 10.00 = 0.12450 mol/L.
  2. δC(HCl) = 0.0002 / 0.1000 · 100% = 0.20%.
  3. δV(HCl) = 0.05 / 12.45 · 100% = 0.40%.
  4. δV(NaOH) = 0.02 / 10.00 · 100% = 0.20%.
  5. Maximum: δ = 0.20 + 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.80%, ΔC = 0.12450 · 0.0080 = 0.0010 mol/L.
  6. Root-sum-square: δ = √(0.20² + 0.40² + 0.20²) = 0.49%, ΔC = 0.0006 mol/L.

Answer. C(NaOH) = 0.1245 ± 0.0010 mol/L (maximum estimate) or 0.1245 ± 0.0006 mol/L (root-sum-square).

Conclusion from the example: the weakest link is the small titrant volume. If the aliquot is chosen so that about 20 mL is used for titration, δV(HCl) drops to 0.05 / 20 · 100% = 0.25%. This is why titrant volumes are kept within 50–90% of the burette capacity; practical tips are given in the guide on how to titrate correctly with a burette.

Example 5: Random Error from a Series of Replicates

Problem. Five titrations gave volumes of 12.45, 12.50, 12.40, 12.48, 12.42 mL. Find the confidence interval of the mean at P = 0.95.

Solution.

  1. Mean: x̄ = (12.45 + 12.50 + 12.40 + 12.48 + 12.42) / 5 = 12.45 mL.
  2. Standard deviation: s = √(Σ(xᵢ - x̄)² / (n - 1)) = 0.041 mL.
  3. Standard deviation of the mean: s / √n = 0.041 / √5 = 0.018 mL.
  4. Student's t for n - 1 = 4 and P = 0.95: t = 2.78.
  5. Δ = t · s / √n = 2.78 · 0.018 = 0.05 mL.

Answer. V = 12.45 ± 0.05 mL (n = 5, P = 0.95), relative standard deviation 0.33%. To include both random and instrumental components, combine them in quadrature: √(0.05² + 0.03²) = 0.06 mL.

Significant Figures in Chemistry: Counting Rules

Significant figures in chemistry are all the digits of a number starting from the first nonzero digit on the left. They show the precision with which a quantity was measured.

  • 0.0250 has three significant figures (leading zeros do not count, trailing zero after the decimal point does).
  • 12.45 mL has four significant figures; writing 12.4 mL means worse precision.
  • 1500 mg is ambiguous; write 1.50 · 10³ mg if the precision is three digits.
  • Exact numbers from definitions (5 replicates, the coefficient 2 in a reaction equation) are exact and do not affect the number of significant figures.

In practice, significant figures in chemistry determine how many digits to write in the answer after calculations. Rules for operations:

  • Multiplication and division: the result has as many significant figures as the least precise factor. 0.1000 · 12.45 / 10.00: all have four digits, answer 0.1245.
  • Addition and subtraction: round the result to the decimal place of the least precise term. 25.4312 g - 25.1 g = 0.3 g, not 0.3312 g.
  • Intermediate calculations carry one or two extra digits and only the final result is rounded. Early rounding accumulates its own error.

How to Round a Measurement Result and Format Your Answer

The question "how to round a measurement result" is solved in two steps, and the order matters: first the error, then the result.

  1. Round the error to one significant figure. If the first significant digit is 1 or 2, keep two: 0.0613 → 0.06; 0.0149 → 0.015; 0.00069 → 0.0007.
  2. Round the result to the same decimal place as the last digit of the error: 0.124503 ± 0.0006 → 0.1245 ± 0.0006.
  3. Write the units once outside the parentheses or after both numbers: (0.1245 ± 0.0006) mol/L.
  4. State the estimation conditions: maximum or root-sum-square, number of trials n, and confidence level P if Student's t was used.
  5. Add the relative error: δ = 0.5%. One or two significant figures are sufficient for relative error.

Final format for the result x ± Δx: C(NaOH) = (0.1245 ± 0.0006) mol/L, δ = 0.5% (root-sum-square estimate from glassware tolerances). For a result from a series of trials, state n and P as in Example 5: V = (12.45 ± 0.05) mL, n = 5, P = 0.95. Incorrect formats the instructor will return for correction: "0.124503 ± 0.000613" (too many digits), "0.12 ± 0.0006" (result rounded more coarsely than the error), "0.1245 ± 0.0006" without units.

Common Student Mistakes

Most of the time, the absolute and relative errors in a report are wrong not because of the arithmetic, but because of incorrectly chosen initial Δ values.

  • Using the scale division instead of the tolerance. The tolerance for a 100 mL volumetric flask is 0.10 mL, not "1 division": a volumetric flask has no divisions at all.
  • Forgetting that weighing by difference means two weighings. Δm = 0.0002 g, not 0.0001 g.
  • Adding absolute errors for a quotient. You can't add grams and milliliters; for products and quotients, only relative errors work.
  • Confusing precision with accuracy. Good precision among replicates doesn't rule out systematic error—that only shows up with a standard sample.
  • Rounding at every step. Keep intermediate values with extra digits, and only start thinking about how to round the measurement result at the very end of the calculation.
  • Writing the error with three or four significant figures. The error itself is known only roughly; more than two digits in it is meaningless.
  • Using class B glassware but reporting the class A tolerance. Check the marking on the specific flask or pipette; you can find the right class in the laboratory glassware section.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should the report state absolute or relative error?

Both. Absolute error is given when writing the result as x ± Δx with units, relative error is given separately as a percentage. Together, absolute and relative error give the reviewer the full picture: how far off the number is and whether that's a lot or a little.

Should the error of the molar mass be taken into account?

Usually not. If you take M to two decimal places (105.99 g/mol), its contribution is thousandths of a percent—tens of times smaller than the flask tolerance. Five significant figures is enough.

How many replicate runs are needed?

To estimate random error in a lab experiment, you need at least three runs. With two runs, the Student coefficient is 12.71 and the interval comes out wider than the instrument error itself. Five runs is a reasonable compromise between time and reliability of the estimate.

Can the systematic error of glassware be reduced?

Yes—calibrate it yourself: measure out water, weigh it on an analytical balance, convert mass to volume using the density of water at the experiment temperature, and then use the actual volume instead of the nominal one.

What should I do if the result differs from the true value by more than the calculated error?

If the calculated error doesn't cover the discrepancy, then not all systematic and random errors have been accounted for: look for the cause in the procedure. Check the titer of the standard solution, the indicator, the temperature of the solutions, the cleanliness of the burette, and redo the calculation with the formulas.

The main thing to remember: the Δ of each measurement comes from the glassware tolerance and the balance resolution; for products and quotients you add relative errors; the error is rounded to one or two significant figures, and the result to the same decimal place. Before your next lab, copy the tolerances of your flask, pipette, and burette into your notebook—then calculating the error in your lab report will take five minutes.
