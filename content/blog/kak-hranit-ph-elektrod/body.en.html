How to store a pH electrode: always keep it moist, in a protective cap filled with 3 mol/L potassium chloride solution or a ready-made storage solution. For a few hours between measurement series, pH 4 or pH 7 buffer will do. Distilled water is not suitable for storing a combination electrode: it leaches KCl from the junction and reference electrolyte, and the electrode starts responding slowly and drifting. An electrode that has been left dry can usually be restored—soak it in 3 mol/L KCl for at least 24 hours and check the slope with two buffers.

Why a pH Electrode Must Not Be Kept Dry

A combination pH electrode is two electrodes in one body. The measuring part is a thin glass membrane with a hydrated (gel) layer on its surface. This is where hydrogen ions exchange with the solution, and this is what produces a potential proportional to pH. The second part is the reference electrode, usually silver/silver chloride, immersed in KCl solution. It connects to the sample through a junction—porous ceramic, ground glass, or fiber—through which the electrolyte slowly leaks out.

When the electrode dries out, both parts suffer:

  • the glass gel layer loses water, and the membrane responds slowly until the layer is restored;
  • KCl crystallizes in the junction pores, contact resistance increases, and readings become noisy and drift;
  • if the reference electrolyte has partially evaporated, bubbles and crystals form inside, and the reference potential shifts.

So when deciding how to store a pH electrode, stick to one rule: the membrane and junction must stay immersed in a solution with a high KCl concentration. The closer it matches the internal electrolyte, the fewer ions leach out of the junction.

Storage Solution for pH Electrode: What to Put in the Cap

In short, how to store a pH electrode: the base solution is 3 mol/L KCl. For electrodes with liquid electrolyte, the most accurate approach is to fill the cap with the same electrolyte that is inside the electrode. If the internal electrolyte is special, such as lithium chloride in alcohol or acetic acid for non-aqueous media, store the electrode in that too, not in aqueous KCl. Ready-made formulations such as L9114 electrolyte solution for regeneration and storage of pH electrodes are convenient because you don't have to prepare and verify the solution yourself.

Can You Store a pH Electrode in Distilled Water?

For a combination electrode—no. The reason is the concentration difference. Inside the electrode and in the junction pores there is 3 mol/L KCl; outside in distilled water there is almost none. K⁺ and Cl⁻ ions continuously diffuse outward, the junction fills with dilute solution, and then the reference electrolyte at the silver/silver chloride element is diluted as well.

The potential of a silver/silver chloride electrode depends on chloride ion activity:

E = E° - (2.303·R·T / F) · lg a(Cl⁻)

where E is the reference electrode potential, mV; E° is the standard potential of the Ag/AgCl couple; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is temperature, K; F = 96485 C/mol; a(Cl⁻) is chloride ion activity. At 25 °C the factor 2.303·R·T / F equals 59.16 mV.

Example 1. How will the reference potential change if the KCl concentration near the silver/silver chloride element drops from 3 to 0.3 mol/L (assuming activity is proportional to concentration)?

Solution: ΔE = 59.16 · lg(3 / 0.3) = 59.16 · lg 10 = 59.16 · 1 = 59.2 mV. Even a drop from 3 to 1 mol/L gives 59.16 · lg 3 = 28.2 mV.

Answer: a shift of about 59 mV—roughly one pH unit. In practice, dilution is slow and uneven, so the meter shows not a jump but a steady drift that calibration removes only briefly. Rinsing the electrode with distilled water between samples is fine and necessary—that's seconds, during which the electrolyte doesn't have time to escape. Leaving it in water overnight is a mistake.

How to Store a pH Electrode Between Measurements and During Breaks

The procedure after finishing work is the same for any combination electrode:

  1. Immediately after the last sample, rinse the electrode with distilled water from a wash bottle. If the sample was fatty or protein-rich, first clean the electrode with an appropriate solution, then rinse.
  2. Remove droplets by blotting the tip with a lint-free wipe. Do not rub the membrane: friction causes static charge and scratches, after which readings take a long time to stabilize.
  3. Check the cap: there should be enough solution to fully immerse the membrane and junction. Replace cloudy storage solution or solution with sediment.
  4. For an electrode with liquid electrolyte, close the fill hole with a plug or slide the sleeve—so the electrolyte doesn't evaporate or leak out unnecessarily.
  5. Put on the cap and place the electrode vertically in a stand, membrane down.
  6. Store at room temperature, away from heaters and direct sunlight; do not allow freezing.
  7. Replace the storage solution in the cap regularly—about once every one to two weeks, and buffer, if used instead of KCl, daily.

How to store a pH electrode if it won't be needed for a month or longer? The same way: cap with KCl, closed fill hole, box. Before returning to service, top up the electrolyte, let the electrode stand in KCl for 1–2 hours, and calibrate.

KCl Crystals on the Electrode: Is It Dangerous?

White deposit around the fill hole, on the sleeve, or on the cap walls is potassium chloride that has leaked out and dried. KCl crystals on the electrode by themselves are not harmful and do not indicate a breakdown: this is how any electrode with liquid electrolyte looks, especially in dry air. Before measurement, rinse off the deposit with warm distilled water and blot the body so salt doesn't get into the sample and spoil the result.

It's worse when crystals are visible inside the reference chamber or in the junction itself. This means the electrolyte has partially evaporated or become supersaturated on cooling. What to do:

  • crystals inside—place the electrode in a water bath at about 45 °C until the salt completely dissolves, then replace the electrolyte with fresh;
  • clogged junction—soak the tip in warm 3 mol/L KCl until resistance is restored and readings stop being noisy;
  • never scrape the junction or membrane with a needle, knife, or sandpaper: mechanical cleaning usually kills the electrode permanently.

How to Refill Electrolyte in a pH Electrode

Only electrodes with a fill hole can be refilled. Gel and polymer electrodes are maintenance-free: the electrolyte in them is not changed, which makes storage in a moist cap even more important. Procedure for a refillable electrode:

  1. Check the body or datasheet for the required electrolyte. Most often it is 3 mol/L KCl, sometimes KCl with added AgCl or a non-aqueous electrolyte. Do not use saturated KCl instead of 3 mol/L: the reference potential will change, and a new calibration will be required.
  2. Open the fill hole.
  3. If the old electrolyte is cloudy, colored, or has sediment, withdraw it with a syringe or pipette, rinse the chamber 1–2 times with fresh electrolyte, and only then fill.
  4. Fill the chamber so the level is about 1 cm below the hole.
  5. Shake out air bubbles, as with a medical thermometer: a bubble at the junction or at the silver/silver chloride element breaks the electrical circuit.
  6. Wipe the body, rinse off spilled electrolyte.
  7. During calibration and measurement, keep the hole open and the electrolyte level above the sample level. Then the electrolyte flows out through the junction under its own pressure, and the sample does not flow in. Close the hole during storage.

Example 2. You need to prepare 250 mL of 3 mol/L KCl solution for refilling and caps. M(KCl) = 74.55 g/mol.

Solution: m = C · V · M = 3 mol/L · 0.250 L · 74.55 g/mol = 55.91 g.

Answer: weigh 55.91 g KCl, dissolve in about 150–200 mL distilled water in a 250 mL volumetric flask, bring to the mark at room temperature, and mix. For 100 mL you need 22.37 g, for 500 mL—111.83 g. Use reagent grade of at least analytical purity: impurities in the reference electrolyte immediately affect the potential. Regeneration of a pH Electrode After Dry Storage

The situation is familiar: the electrode was found in a cabinet without a cap, or the cap has dried out. pH electrode regeneration proceeds from gentle to harsh measures, and after each step the electrode is checked against buffers:

  1. Inspect the electrode. A crack or chip in the membrane, a broken body—there is nothing to restore. A cloudy membrane and KCl crystals on the electrode are a normal picture after drying.
  2. Rinse off crystals with warm distilled water. For a refillable electrode, replace the electrolyte with fresh and remove bubbles—just as in normal refilling: how to refill electrolyte in a pH electrode without bubbles is covered in the previous section.
  3. Soak the electrode in 3 mol/L KCl for at least 24 hours. For a gel electrode, this is the main and almost the only way to restore it.
  4. Calibrate with two buffers and check the slope, zero offset, and response time.
  5. If the response is still slow, hold the membrane for a few minutes in 0.1 mol/L HCl at room temperature; if the deposit doesn't come off, the solution can be carefully warmed, but not above 50 °C. Then rinse and keep the electrode in KCl for at least an hour.
  6. For known contamination, choose cleaning accordingly: proteins are removed with pepsin solution in hydrochloric acid in about an hour; black ceramic junction from sulfides—with 7.5% thiourea in 0.1 mol/L HCl until discoloration disappears; fats and oils—with ethanol or acetone, if the plastic body can withstand them.
  7. After any chemical cleaning—rinse, at least an hour in electrolyte, and mandatory recalibration.

Do not clean the inner chamber of the reference electrode with chemicals: this irreversibly damages the silver/silver chloride element. Remove internal dirt only by changing the electrolyte, if necessary warm, about 45 °C. How to Check the Electrode After Restoration: Calculation Examples

pH electrode regeneration is complete only when calibration confirms it. Slope is calculated from potentials in two buffers:

S = (E₁ - E₂) / (pH₁ - pH₂), S, % = |S| / S(theor) · 100, S(theor) = 2.303 · R · T / F = 0.1984 · T

where E₁, E₂ are potentials in buffers, mV; pH₁, pH₂ are buffer values at calibration temperature; T is temperature, K; S(theor) is theoretical slope, mV/pH. At 20 °C it is 58.17 mV/pH, at 25 °C—59.16 mV/pH, at 37 °C—61.54 mV/pH.

Example 3. After a day in KCl, the electrode at 25 °C showed -5 mV in pH 7.00 buffer and +166 mV in pH 4.01 buffer. Is it suitable?

Solution: S = (166 - (-5)) / (4.01 - 7.00) = 171 / (-2.99) = -57.19 mV/pH. S, % = 57.19 / 59.16 · 100 = 96.7%. Zero offset—potential at pH 7—is -5 mV.

Answer: slope 96.7%, offset -5 mV, the electrode is restored and suitable for use.

Example 4. Before regeneration, the same electrode at 25 °C gave a slope of 52.3 mV/pH and zero offset +40 mV. How to assess its condition?

Solution: S, % = 52.3 / 59.16 · 100 = 88.4%. An offset of 40 mV corresponds to 40 / 59.16 = 0.68 pH units that the meter has to compensate for during calibration.

Answer: the slope is below 90% and the zero offset is large — an electrode in this state is no good for accurate measurements; this is the typical picture after dry storage, and regenerating the pH electrode brought it back to normal.

Example 5. Calibration at 20 °C: pH 7.00 buffer (its value at 20 °C is 7.02) gave +3 mV, pH 4.01 buffer (at 20 °C — 4.00) gave +172 mV.

Solution: S = (172 - 3) / (4.00 - 7.02) = 169 / (-3.02) = -55.96 mV/pH. Compare with the theoretical slope at 20 °C: 55.96 / 58.17 · 100 = 96.2%.

Answer: 96.2%, the electrode is fine. A common mistake is dividing by 59.16 at any temperature: you get 94.6%, and a perfectly good electrode gets sent off for cleaning for no reason. Modern pH meters compensate for temperature automatically, but if you calculate by hand you absolutely must factor it in.

Common storage and maintenance mistakes

A slow response almost always comes down not to a defect but to the fact that nobody showed the beginner how to store a pH electrode.

  • A cap filled with distilled water "to keep it moist" is the most common cause of slow response and drift.
  • The cap is on, but the solution inside dried up long ago. Check the level at least once a week.
  • pH 7 buffer left in the cap for weeks: the solution turns cloudy and a biofilm forms on the membrane.
  • The fill hole is closed during measurement — the electrolyte can't flow and the readings are unstable.
  • Saturated KCl or "whatever electrolyte was on hand" instead of the one specified for the electrode.
  • Measuring samples right after regeneration or cleaning without recalibrating.

What you'll need for storage and regeneration

The minimum kit for electrode care: a storage cap (the original one from the box), 3 mol/L KCl solution or a ready-made pH electrode storage solution, pH 4 and pH 7 buffers, 0.1 mol/L HCl, a wash bottle, lint-free wipes, a 250 mL volumetric flask and scales, a syringe for replacing the electrolyte, beakers and a stand. If after all these steps the slope still won't climb above 90%, it makes more sense to replace the electrode — for example with a combined SenTix 41 pH electrode — and to store it by the book from day one.

Frequently asked questions

Can a pH electrode be stored in distilled water overnight?

A combined electrode — not advisable even overnight: in 10-12 hours a noticeable amount of KCl leaks out through the junction, and in the morning the electrode takes longer to settle on stable readings. If there's no other option, in the morning soak it for 1-2 hours in 3 mol/L KCl and calibrate.

What can replace pH electrode storage solution if you've run out?

The best choice is 3 mol/L KCl that you make yourself. Until the end of the workday, pH 4 or pH 7 buffer will do; overnight and longer — only KCl. Tap water — only for a few hours; distilled water — not at all.

How long should an electrode sit in KCl after dry storage?

At least 24 hours. An electrode that was dry for only a short time often recovers within a few hours, but you need to check with a calibration, not by feel.

How often does a pH electrode need regeneration?

With proper storage — only when calibration shows the slope dropping below 95% or a response slower than 30 s. In dirty matrices (milk, meat, wastewater), cleaning is done on a schedule, for example once a week.

How long does a pH electrode last?

In the lab, usually one to three years, and the lifespan depends heavily on whether the lab technician knows how to store the pH electrode between jobs. Glass ages even when idle, so a spare electrode should also be kept in a cap with KCl.

The question of how to store a pH electrode boils down to three things: a cap with 3 mol/L KCl instead of water, the fill hole open during measurement and closed during storage, and 24 hours in KCl plus calibration for a dried-out electrode. That's enough to keep the electrode working fast and stable for its entire service life.
