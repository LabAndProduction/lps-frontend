How to choose a syringe filter: first look at what the sample is, then pick the membrane, pore size, and diameter. For aqueous samples and water-organic mixtures, the most convenient choice is a hydrophilic membrane made of regenerated cellulose (RC) or nylon; for pure organic solvents, aggressive acids, and gases, use PTFE; for proteins, use low-binding membranes (RC, PES, cellulose acetate) but not nylon. A 0.45 µm pore is sufficient for standard HPLC on columns with 3–5 µm particles; a 0.22 µm pore is needed for UHPLC on sub-2 µm particles and for sterilizing filtration.

Below is a step-by-step guide to choosing a syringe filter for a specific sample, compatibility tables, pressure and sample volume calculations, and mistakes that cause the filter to clog, leak, or skew results.

How to Choose a Syringe Filter in Five Steps

  1. Identify the sample solvent. Water and buffers, water-methanol or water-acetonitrile mixtures, pure organics (hexane, THF, chloroform), concentrated acid—check membrane-solvent compatibility and choose the material accordingly.
  2. Consider what you're analyzing. Proteins and peptides stick to nylon; trace metals can leach from some membranes and housings; and trace amounts of drug substances sometimes adsorb onto the membrane.
  3. Choose the pore size. 0.45 µm for clarification and HPLC on standard columns; 0.22 µm for UHPLC, sterility, and microbiology.
  4. Choose the diameter by volume. Less than 1 mL—4 mm; 1–10 mL—13 mm; 10–100 mL—25 mm.
  5. Decide if sterility is needed. For cultures and media, use sterile filters in individual packaging; for analytical sample prep, non-sterile is sufficient.

Hydrophilic vs Hydrophobic Membranes: What's the Difference

Hydrophilic and hydrophobic membranes differ in how water wets them. A hydrophilic membrane (nylon, RC, PES, cellulose acetate) wets on its own: aqueous solutions pass immediately under low pressure. A hydrophobic membrane (standard PTFE, polypropylene) repels water, and dry PTFE barely lets aqueous samples through no matter how hard you push. However, organic solvents and gases pass through easily.

Hence the practical rule. If you need to filter an aqueous sample through PTFE, pre-wet the membrane: pass 0.5–1 mL of methanol or isopropanol, then 1–2 mL of water to displace the alcohol, and only then the sample, avoiding air entry. There is also hydrophilic PTFE that wets with water without alcohol—labeled as hydrophilic on the packaging.

The reverse is true for hydrophilic membranes: a wetted 0.2 µm membrane won't let air through until pressure exceeds several bar. So an air bubble between the plunger and the sample after wetting the membrane won't be pushed through, and it seems like the filter is clogged.

Check membrane-solvent compatibility before filtration, not after: an incompatible membrane swells, tears, or leaches extractables into the sample, which then show up as extra peaks on the chromatogram. Most analytical filter housings are polypropylene, which resists most solvents, so the membrane is the deciding factor.

Syringe Filter 0.22 vs 0.45 µm: When to Use Which

Choose a 0.22 or 0.45 µm syringe filter based on what happens to the sample next. A 0.45 µm pore retains particles that clog the inlet frit of columns with 3–5 µm sorbent particles, and it filters faster. A 0.22 µm pore also retains bacteria, so it's used for sterilizing filtration and for UHPLC systems where sub-2 µm columns and very narrow capillaries are sensitive to fine particulates.

ApplicationPoreWhy
HPLC, 3–5 µm columns0.45 µmSufficient for column protection, higher flow
UHPLC, sub-2 µm columns0.22 µmFrits and capillaries clog with fine particulates
Dissolved metals for ICP and AAS0.45 µmAccepted boundary between dissolved and suspended forms
Sterilization of media, antibiotic solutions0.22 µm, sterileRetains bacteria
Clarification of turbid extractsGlass fiber prefilter + 0.45 µmOtherwise the filter clogs after 1–2 mL

A common mistake is using 0.22 µm everywhere "just in case." For standard HPLC this offers no benefit, and turbid samples are harder to push through a fine pore and clog the membrane faster. The most reliable column protection is a guard column and clean mobile phase: sample particulates are one of the common causes of why pressure rises in HPLC.

Syringe Filter 13 vs 25 mm and How Much Sample to Draw

Choose the diameter by sample volume. A larger filter has more membrane area, so it filters faster and clogs later, but it also retains more sample (the so-called dead volume).

DiameterMembrane Area, Approx.Sample VolumeDead Volume After Air Purge
4 mm~0.07 cm²Up to 1 mL~10 µL
13 mm~1 cm²1–10 mLUp to 100 µL
25 mm~4.8 cm²10–100 mLUp to 200 µL

Example 1. Precious 0.5 mL sample. Condition: 0.5 mL extract, choosing between 25 mm and 4 mm filters. Solution: loss on a 25 mm filter is 0.2 / 0.5 × 100 = 40%, so 0.5 − 0.2 = 0.3 mL reaches the vial. On a 4 mm filter, loss is 0.01 / 0.5 × 100 = 2%, so 0.49 mL reaches the vial. Answer: for small volumes, use a 4 mm filter.

Example 2. How much to draw into the syringe. Condition: need 1.0 mL filtrate in the vial, 13 mm filter, discard the first 0.5 mL to waste to rinse off membrane extractables. Solution: V = V_vial + V_discard + V_dead = 1.0 + 0.5 + 0.1 = 1.6 mL. Answer: draw at least 1.6 mL, practically 2 mL.

Example 3. Turbid extract. Condition: 13 mm filter clogs after about 5 mL of sample. Solution: throughput is roughly proportional to membrane area: 5 × 4.8 / 1.0 = 24 mL. Answer: a 25 mm filter will pass about 24 mL of the same sample, roughly five times more.

Bottom line: choose a 13 or 25 mm syringe filter by volume—for a single HPLC vial (1–2 mL), 13 mm is sufficient; for turbid samples and volumes from 10 mL, use 25 mm. 25 mm filters with various membranes, such as the Minisart RC 25 regenerated cellulose unit, are handy as universal filters for HPLC and protein samples.

Why It's Hard to Push a Syringe Filter

The pressure generated by hand is calculated by the formula:

p = F / A, where p is pressure, Pa; F is force on the plunger, N; A is plunger area, m² (A = π × d² / 4, d is the syringe inner diameter, m). 1 bar = 100,000 Pa.

Example 4. Condition: the analyst pushes the plunger with about 40 N (roughly 4 kgf). Inner diameter of a 1 mL syringe is about 4.7 mm, 5 mL—about 12 mm, 20 mL—about 20 mm. Solution: for a 5 mL syringe, A = 3.1416 × 0.012² / 4 = 1.131 × 10⁻⁴ m², p = 40 / (1.131 × 10⁻⁴) = 354,000 Pa ≈ 3.5 bar. For a 20 mL syringe, A = 3.1416 × 0.020² / 4 = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m², p = 40 / (3.142 × 10⁻⁴) = 127,000 Pa ≈ 1.27 bar. For a 1 mL syringe, A = 3.1416 × 0.0047² / 4 = 1.73 × 10⁻⁵ m², p = 40 / (1.73 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 2.3 × 10⁶ Pa ≈ 23 bar. Answer: the same force in a large syringe produces 18 times less pressure than in a small one.

This is the first reason it's hard to push a syringe filter: too large a syringe. Hence two conclusions. If the sample is hard to push from a 20 mL syringe, transfer it to a 5 or 10 mL syringe. And with a small 1 mL syringe, it's easy to exceed the filter housing's maximum pressure rating (stated on the packaging)—the housing may crack or pop off the tip.

Why it's hard to push a syringe filter when the syringe is fine:

  • dry hydrophobic PTFE membrane and aqueous sample—needs alcohol wetting;
  • turbid or viscous sample (extracts, syrups, protein solutions)—needs a prefilter, centrifugation before filtration, or a larger diameter filter;
  • 0.22 µm pore where 0.45 µm would suffice;
  • air lock on an already wetted hydrophilic membrane;
  • membrane swelling in an incompatible solvent.

The summary table above answers how to choose a syringe filter for typical lab samples. If you're unsure whether the membrane adsorbs your analyte, run a recovery check: analyze the same sample before and after filtration (centrifuge the unfiltered portion first).

R = C_f / C_0 × 100%, where R is recovery, %; C_f is concentration in the filtrate; C_0 is concentration in the unfiltered sample (in the same units).

Example 5. Condition: 10.0 µg/mL protein standard, 8.6 µg/mL found after nylon filter, 9.9 µg/mL after RC filter. Solution: R(nylon) = 8.6 / 10.0 × 100 = 86%, R(RC) = 9.9 / 10.0 × 100 = 99%. Answer: nylon loses 14% protein; for this sample, use RC. In method development, recovery is typically targeted at 98–102%.

Common Filtration Mistakes

Even when you know how to choose a syringe filter, mistakes during filtration can ruin results.

  • Not checking membrane-solvent compatibility for a new sample. Extra peaks on the chromatogram often come from membrane extractables.
  • Drawing the sample into the syringe with the filter attached. When drawing through the filter, particles deposit on the outlet side of the membrane, and when pushing, the flow washes them into the vial. Also, backflow can detach the membrane from its support. Remove the filter, draw the sample, then reattach the filter.
  • Not discarding the first portion. The first 0.5–1 mL rinses extractables from the membrane and saturates binding sites. For trace analysis, this is essential.
  • Using one filter for multiple samples. Cross-contamination is inevitable; filters are single-use.
  • Confusing hydrophilic and hydrophobic PTFE. The label determines whether alcohol wetting is needed.
  • Filtering proteins through nylon and glass fiber. Results are underestimated with no visible signs.
  • Pushing the plunger in jerks. A sudden pressure spike ruptures the membrane, especially on 1–3 mL syringes. Press smoothly and evenly.

You'll need disposable syringes without a rubber seal, or with an inert plunger (rubber leaches impurities into organic solvents), autosampler vials, a waste container for the discarded portion, and a centrifuge for cloudy samples. Frequently asked questions

Can I filter an aqueous sample through hydrophobic PTFE?

Yes, if you first wet the membrane with 0.5–1 mL of methanol or isopropanol and then rinse it with water. Without wetting, water won't pass through dry hydrophobic PTFE at the pressure your hand can generate.

Do I need to filter a sample if it looks clear?

For HPLC, yes: particles 1–5 µm in size are invisible to the eye, but they're exactly what settles on the column frit. The exception is samples that have already passed through a 0.45 µm filter or been centrifuged at high speed.

How do I choose a syringe filter when I don't know what's in the sample?

Go with a hydrophilic RC (regenerated cellulose) filter with a 0.45 µm pore: it's compatible with water and most organic solvents and binds proteins weakly. Then compare the recovery of the filtered and unfiltered sample and switch the membrane if needed. For UHPLC and sterility, choose 0.22 µm.

Can I autoclave a non-sterile syringe filter?

Many filters with RC, PTFE, and nylon membranes withstand autoclaving at 121 °C, but you need to check the instructions for the specific filter. For microbiology, it's safer to just use filters supplied sterile.

The main thing to remember about choosing a syringe filter: choose the membrane by solvent and analyte (check the membrane's solvent compatibility in advance), the pore size by column or sterility requirement, and the diameter by sample volume. If you keep nylon or RC for aqueous samples, PTFE for organics, and a couple of sterile 0.22 µm filters for microbiology in the lab, that's enough for most everyday tasks, and for an unusual sample, a recovery check before and after filtration will help.
