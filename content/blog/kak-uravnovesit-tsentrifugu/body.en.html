To understand how to balance a centrifuge, one rule suffices: tubes placed opposite each other or forming a symmetrical group must have equal mass, not equal volume. Tubes are weighed together with caps and adapters, pairs are equalized on a scale to 0.1 g (unless the rotor manual requires tighter), and for an odd number of samples add a counterweight with water or arrange tubes in a triangle if the rotor allows. Below: calculation of imbalance force, loading schemes for 6-, 8-, 12-, and 24-place rotors, and an analysis of why a centrifuge shakes.

Why rotor imbalance is so dangerous

While the rotor is stationary, an extra gram in one tube means nothing. During rotation, that gram pulls the axis sideways with a force that grows with the square of speed:

F = Δm · ω² · r, where ω = 2π · n / 60

  • F – unbalanced force, N;
  • Δm – mass difference between opposite tubes, kg;
  • ω – angular velocity, rad/s; n – RPM;
  • r – radius of rotation of the tube center, m.

If the relative centrifugal force (RCF) is known (how many times centrifugal acceleration exceeds g), the formula simplifies: F = Δm · g · RCF, where g = 9.81 m/s².

Example 1. A benchtop centrifuge with an 8-place angle rotor, 6000 rpm, RCF = 3461 (radius about 8.6 cm). One tube is 1 g heavier than the opposite one.

  1. Convert mass: Δm = 1 g = 0.001 kg.
  2. F = 0.001 · 9.81 · 3461 = 34 N.
  3. 34 N / 9.81 = 3.5 kg – as if a 3.5 kg weight were hung on the axis sideways and rotates at 100 revolutions per second.

Answer: about 34 N. With a 0.1 g difference the force drops tenfold to 3.4 N, and the benchtop centrifuge drive handles it without vibration. Hence the practical tolerance of 0.1 g: if you balance a benchtop centrifuge this way, it runs smoothly and quietly. For high-speed and ultracentrifuges the tolerance is tighter and always specified in the rotor manual.

Systematic rotor imbalance damages bearings and drive dampers, causes fatigue cracks in the rotor and vibration that resuspends the pellet. In the worst case a tube or rotor bursts, which is why the centrifuge housing is designed to contain debris.

How to balance a centrifuge – step-by-step

  1. Check the rotor and tubes: the rotor body shows maximum speed, tubes show maximum RCF. Ordinary glass tubes are rated to about 4000 g; higher speeds require polypropylene or special glass.
  2. Use tubes of the same type and manufacturer in one run: empty tubes from different brands differ in mass by tenths of a gram or more – already above the 0.1 g tolerance.
  3. Fill tubes outside the centrifuge and not above the maximum mark. In an angle rotor the liquid surface is slanted, and overfilling causes leakage even under the cap.
  4. Weigh each tube with cap and adapter on a balance with 0.01 g resolution. It is convenient to place the tube in a beaker or rack after taring them.
  5. Pair tubes of similar mass and equalize each pair to 0.1 g: add a little buffer or solvent with a pipette to the lighter tube, or use a counterweight.
  6. Place pairs strictly opposite each other. For an odd number of samples, use a symmetrical scheme from the section below or add a counterweight.
  7. For a bucket rotor, hang all buckets, even empty ones, and check that they swing freely.
  8. Close the rotor lid (if present) and the centrifuge lid, start the program, and stay nearby for the first 20–30 seconds of acceleration. If you hear knocking, humming, or the centrifuge moves across the bench, stop it with the Stop button and find out why the centrifuge vibrates before restarting.

Balancing tubes in a centrifuge by mass, not volume

Equal volume gives equal mass only when liquids have the same density. In the lab this is rarely the case: blood, sucrose gradients, organic solvents, and suspensions are noticeably heavier than water. Therefore balancing tubes by the marks on the tube is a common cause of imbalance.

Example 2. You need to centrifuge one tube with 8 mL of chloroform extract. The sample is in a glass tube with a cap (chloroform softens polypropylene); the empty tube weighs 6.5 g. How much water to put in the counterweight?

  1. Mass of chloroform: m = V · ρ = 8 · 1.48 = 11.84 g.
  2. Mass of tube with sample: 6.5 + 11.84 = 18.34 g.
  3. In an identical empty tube you need 11.84 g of water: V = 11.84 / 0.998 = 11.9 mL.

Answer: about 11.9 mL of water, not 8 mL. If you eyeball 8 mL, the difference is almost 4 g. In practice you don't calculate the volume: place the counterweight on the balance and add water with a pipette until it reaches the same 18.34 g.

Example 3. Two blood tubes are opposite each other: one has 6 mL, the other 4 mL. What force arises at RCF = 3461?

  1. Volume difference 2 mL, mass difference Δm = 2 · 1.06 = 2.12 g = 0.00212 kg.
  2. F = 0.00212 · 9.81 · 3461 = 72 N, i.e. about 7.3 kg of lateral force.

Answer: 72 N. The solution is not to pair such tubes. Sort all tubes by mass and pair adjacent weights, and balance the single one with a counterweight. Even 5 mL of blood versus 5 mL of water gives a difference of 5.30 – 4.99 = 0.31 g, i.e. 10.5 N, so the counterweight for blood is also adjusted by weight.

Odd number of tubes in a centrifuge – loading schemes

The rotor is balanced when the center of mass of the entire load lies on the axis. The opposite pair is the simplest way to achieve this, but not the only one: three identical tubes at equal intervals (a triangle) are also balanced. Therefore some odd numbers (3, 5, 7...) are acceptable without a counterweight if the number of wells is divisible by 3. The exception is N – 1 tubes: one empty well cannot be balanced by anything.

Verification rule: k tubes of equal mass can be placed in an N-place rotor without a counterweight if both k and the number of empty wells N – k can be composed of symmetrical groups (pairs, triplets, fives – divisors of N that are prime numbers). Below is what happens for typical rotors and how to balance the centrifuge in each case.

Number the wells clockwise from 1 to N (many rotors have numbers stamped). Proven schemes:

  • 6 places, 3 tubes: wells 1, 3, 5. Five tubes – only with a sixth counterweight tube.
  • 8 places, odd number: a triangle is impossible; a counterweight is needed. Three tubes + counterweight = 1-5 and 3-7 (cross). Five + counterweight = 1-5, 2-6, 3-7.
  • 12 places, 5 tubes: triangle 1, 5, 9 and pair 2-8. Seven tubes: 1, 5, 9, 2-8, 4-10.
  • 24 places, 5 tubes: triangle 1, 9, 17 and pair 4-16. Seven tubes: 1, 9, 17, 3-15, 6-18.

Important caveat: within a triangle all three tubes must have the same mass, not just "opposite" ones. For a 24-place rotor this is convenient: in a 24-place angle rotor any 2–22 tubes can be arranged into triplets and pairs. If sample masses differ, it is simpler and more reliable to work in pairs and add a centrifuge counterweight – one tube with water.

Example 4. In an 8-place rotor, 7 tubes of 12.40 g each. Place the eighth – a 12.40 g counterweight – and get four pairs 1-5, 2-6, 3-7, 4-8. Without a counterweight, 7 tubes in such a rotor cannot be balanced at all: with eight wells, symmetrical groups are only pairs.

Centrifuge counterweight – how to make one

A centrifuge counterweight is a tube of the same type and size as the sample, with a cap, filled with water to the same mass. For samples denser than water, use a salt or glycerol solution: this way the liquid level is closer to the sample level, and the center of mass is at the same radius.

  • Use a tube from the same manufacturer: for example, 15 mL conical polypropylene tubes are convenient to keep as a separate labeled batch "counterweight".
  • Weigh on a balance with 0.01 g resolution. For this task, precision balances are sufficient; analytical balances are not necessary.
  • Label the counterweight with a marker ("H₂O, 18.34 g") and store it in a rack next to the centrifuge. Re-weigh before each run: water evaporates even from a closed tube.
  • Do not make a counterweight from an empty tube with a piece of plasticine or a coin: the weight sits at the bottom, its center of mass is at a different radius, and during rotation its force does not match the sample's force.

Loading an angle and bucket rotor

Loading an angle and bucket rotor follows the same principle – mass symmetry about the axis – but their errors differ.

Angle rotor

Wells are rigidly fixed at an angle of 25–50°. Tubes are placed in pairs or symmetrical groups; all adapters in the rotor must be of the same type. If an adapter for a small tube is inserted into a well, the same adapter is needed in the opposite well, even if it is empty. Fill no higher than the mark, caps screwed on tightly. The benchtop EBA 200 centrifuge with 8-place angle rotor is a typical example: with 8 wells, an odd number of tubes cannot be balanced without a counterweight.

Bucket rotor (with swinging buckets)

  • All buckets are hung, even if only two are used. A missing bucket means immediate imbalance of tens of grams.
  • Buckets and their adapters are numbered and matched in pairs by mass. Do not swap buckets between rotors and do not use adapters from different sets.
  • Weigh and compare the entire bucket: bucket + adapter + tubes. Opposite buckets are equalized to the tolerance specified in the manual.
  • Inside the bucket, tubes are placed symmetrically about its center. If all tubes are shifted to one side, the bucket does not become horizontal during acceleration and hits the rotor.
  • Lubricate the bucket pivot axes according to the schedule: a bucket that does not swing freely causes imbalance even with perfect weight.

Look for spare buckets, adapters, and lids among accessories for general laboratory equipment – choose them for the rotor model and always in pairs.

Why the centrifuge vibrates or stops with an imbalance error

Modern centrifuges have a vibration sensor: when the threshold is exceeded, "Imbalance" appears on the display and the drive brakes. This message is issued when the load is uneven or there is insufficient lubrication of the axes and suspension. The sensor only stops the unit but does not correct the rotor imbalance. Let's analyze why the centrifuge vibrates by symptoms.

After an error, do not open the lid immediately: wait for the rotor to stop completely. If a tube broke, wait a few more minutes for the aerosol to settle, especially with biological material. Restart only after re-balancing. If the error returns with a balanced load, the centrifuge needs service, not another "restart and hope".

Common balancing mistakes

  • Equalizing by volume marks. Balancing tubes by marks works only for liquids of equal density. For blood, gradients, and solvents – only scales.
  • Weighing without caps. Caps of different tubes weigh differently; in a bucket rotor, adapters too.
  • "Opposite" tube one well off. In a 24-place rotor, opposite well 1 is 13, not 12. Simple rule: opposite well i is i + N/2.
  • Triangle in an 8-place rotor. 360° / 3 = 120°, but wells are spaced 45° apart: there is simply no point at 120°.
  • Different adapters in a pair. Tubes are balanced but adapters are not – the result is the same imbalance.
  • Glass tubes at high speed. Ordinary glass cracks above about 4000 g; shards and liquid cause sudden imbalance.

Frequently Asked Questions

How precisely do tubes need to be balanced in a centrifuge?

For benchtop centrifuges (up to roughly 15,000 rpm), the practical tolerance is 0.1 g per pair, unless the rotor instructions call for something stricter. For high-speed rotors and ultracentrifuges, the exact figure is given in the rotor instructions, and it's tighter.

Can I centrifuge just one tube?

No. A single tube always creates an imbalance, so you need a counterweight of the same mass opposite it. This applies to microcentrifuges for microtubes as well.

Do I need to balance 1.5 mL microtubes?

Yes, but usually equal volumes of aqueous solutions loaded in pairs is enough, since sample mass is small. If the tubes hold solutions of different densities or the volumes differ by more than 0.1 mL, use a counterweight.

Why does the centrifuge vibrate even though the tubes are balanced?

Check whether the adapters in a pair match, whether all buckets are hung properly, whether the rotor nut is tightened, and whether the unit sits on a level, rigid table. If everything checks out and the vibration persists, the shock absorbers or the drive are worn and it needs service.

What should I fill the counterweight with if the sample is denser than water?

Use a salt solution or glycerol of the right density so the liquid level is close to that of the sample. Either way, fine-tune the final mass on a scale.

The key thing to remember about balancing a centrifuge: you balance by mass, not by volume, and pairs go strictly opposite each other. An odd number of tubes is handled with a counterweight or a triangle of pairs if the number of slots is divisible by 3 (except for loading N − 1). Keep a scale with 0.01 g resolution and a labeled counterweight next to the centrifuge, and the Imbalance error will only show up when the centrifuge genuinely needs service.
