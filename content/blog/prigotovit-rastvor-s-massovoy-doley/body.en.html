To understand how to make a solution with a mass fraction w, one formula is enough: the mass of solute equals w × mass of solution, and you add water to bring the solution up to the required mass. For 200 g of a 5% solution, that's 10 g of salt and 190 g of water. If the substance comes as a hydrate (CuSO₄·5H₂O, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O), increase the weighed amount by the mass of water of crystallization, and reduce the water by the same amount. Percent "mass/volume" is a different quantity: it expresses grams of substance per 100 mL of final solution, not per 100 g.

Mass fraction formula and what each letter means

The mass fraction shows what part of the solution's mass is the dissolved substance:

w = m(solute) / m(solution), or in percent w, % = m(solute) / m(solution) × 100

  • m(solute) - mass of the dissolved substance, g;
  • m(solution) - mass of the entire solution, g, i.e. solute plus solvent: m(solution) = m(solute) + m(water);
  • w - dimensionless fraction (0.05) or percentage (5%). In calculations, use the fraction, not the number 5.

This formula gives two working equations used to prepare the solution:

m(solute) = w × m(solution)
m(water) = m(solution) - m(solute)

Percentage concentration in school and university problems almost always means mass fraction in percent. It's convenient to prepare: everything is weighed on a balance, you don't need to know the solution's density, and the result doesn't depend on temperature. Water can also be measured with a cylinder because the density of water at room temperature is close to 1 g/mL (at 20 °C - 0.998 g/mL), so for educational work 190 g of water is 190 mL.

How to make a solution with a mass fraction step by step

For salt, alkali, or sugar the procedure is the same, only the numbers change.

  1. Find the masses. Write down m(solution) and w, find m(solute) and m(water). If the reagent is a hydrate, convert the weighed amount first (see section below).
  2. Prepare the balance. Place it on a level table away from drafts, wait for stable readings, and tare. For weighed amounts from 1 to 100 g, a balance with 0.01 g readability is sufficient for a teaching lab, e.g. Sartorius Entris II precision balances. If the weighed amount is less than 1 g, first make sure it is not below the minimum weight of the balance, otherwise the weighing error becomes a noticeable fraction of the result.
  3. Weigh the substance. Place a dry beaker or weighing bottle on the balance, press "tare", and add the calculated mass with a spatula. Do not return excess to the reagent bottle.
  4. Measure the water. The most convenient way is to pour water directly into the same beaker on the balance after re-taring: this eliminates cylinder error. Alternatively, measure water with a graduated cylinder, assuming 1 mL = 1 g.
  5. Dissolve. Stir with a glass rod, e.g. a 7×200 mm stirring rod, until crystals completely disappear. For 200–300 g of solution, a 500 mL tall-form borosilicate glass beaker works well: it has volume headroom, the solution won't splash out, and the glass withstands heating on a hotplate.
  6. Transfer and label. Pour the solution into a clean bottle and label it: substance, w, date, name.

If dissolution has a noticeable thermal effect (NaOH, H₂SO₄), place the beaker in cold water, and always add acid to water, not the other way around.

Worked examples with numbers

Example 1. How much salt and water for 200 g of 5% solution

Problem. You need 200 g of NaCl solution with a mass fraction of 5%.

Solution. m(NaCl) = 0.05 × 200 = 10 g. m(water) = 200 - 10 = 190 g, about 190 mL.

Answer. 10 g NaCl and 190 g water. For 500 g of solution, simply multiply by 2.5: 25 g salt and 475 g water.

Example 2. How to make 150 g of 10% solution

Problem. You need 150 g of 10% sucrose solution.

Solution. m(sucrose) = 0.10 × 150 = 15 g. m(water) = 150 - 15 = 135 g.

Answer. 15 g sucrose and 135 g water. A common mistake in the problem "how to make a 10% solution" is to dissolve 10 g of substance in 100 g of water. Then the solution mass is 110 g, and w = 10 / 110 × 100 = 9.09%, not 10%.

Example 3. Solution from copper sulfate pentahydrate

When asked how to make a solution with a mass fraction from a crystalline salt, the key is not to confuse the mass of anhydrous substance with the mass of crystals.

Problem. You need 250 g of CuSO₄ solution with a mass fraction of 8%. Only CuSO₄·5H₂O is available.

Solution.

  1. Mass of anhydrous salt in the solution: m(CuSO₄) = 0.08 × 250 = 20 g.
  2. Molar masses: M(CuSO₄) = 159.60 g/mol, M(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = 159.60 + 5 × 18.015 = 249.68 g/mol.
  3. Mass of hydrate: m = 20 × 249.68 / 159.60 = 31.29 g.
  4. Mass of water: 250 - 31.29 = 218.71 g, about 218.7 mL.

Answer. 31.29 g CuSO₄·5H₂O and 218.7 g water. If you mistakenly take 20 g of hydrate and 230 g of water, you get a solution with w = 5.11%, not 8%.

Making a solution from a hydrate

In a hydrate, part of the mass is water of crystallization, which becomes part of the solvent upon dissolution. Therefore, the mass fraction is always calculated on the anhydrous salt, and the weighed amount of hydrate is larger. Making a solution from a hydrate comes down to three formulas:

m(anhydrous salt) = w × m(solution)
m(hydrate) = m(anhydrous salt) × M(hydrate) / M(anhydrous salt)
m(water) = m(solution) - m(hydrate)

The ratio M(hydrate) / M(anhydrous salt) is the conversion factor. It's handy to keep these for reagents commonly encountered in labs:

Example 4. Sodium carbonate solution from washing soda

Problem. You need 200 g of 5% Na₂CO₃ solution, and you have Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.

Solution. m(Na₂CO₃) = 0.05 × 200 = 10 g. m(Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) = 10 × 2.700 = 27.00 g. m(water) = 200 - 27.00 = 173.00 g.

Answer. 27.00 g decahydrate and 173.00 g water. Notice that for the decahydrate the weighed amount is almost three times larger than for the anhydrous salt, and the error from forgetting water of crystallization is especially large here.

Another practical point: hydrates lose water over time (soda and sodium sulfate effloresce and become covered with a white crust), while anhydrous CaCl₂ and NaOH, on the contrary, absorb moisture from the air. For precise work, take the reagent from a tightly closed bottle and weigh quickly.

Diluting a concentrated solution and the cross rule

If the solution is made not from a dry substance but from a more concentrated solution, the mass of solute does not change upon dilution:

w₁ × m₁ = w₂ × m₂

where w₁ and m₁ are the mass fraction and mass of the initial solution, w₂ and m₂ are the same for the desired solution.

Example 5. Making 5% from 20% solution

Problem. You have 20% NaCl solution and need 300 g of 5% solution.

Solution. m₁ = 0.05 × 300 / 0.20 = 75 g of initial solution. Water: 300 - 75 = 225 g. If it's easier to measure the concentrate by volume, divide the mass by density: the density of 20% NaCl at 20 °C is about 1.148 g/mL, so V = 75 / 1.148 = 65.3 mL.

Answer. 75 g (65.3 mL) of 20% solution and 225 g water.

When mixing two solutions of different concentrations, the cross rule (Pearson's square) is convenient. Suppose you have 30% and 5% solutions and need 200 g of 12%. Parts are calculated by differences: take 12 - 5 = 7 parts of 30% solution, 30 - 12 = 18 parts of 5% solution, total 25 parts. Mass of 30% solution: 200 × 7 / 25 = 56 g, of 5% solution: 200 × 18 / 25 = 144 g. Check: (0.30 × 56 + 0.05 × 144) / 200 = 24 / 200 = 0.12, i.e. 12%.

Mass fraction vs. mass-volume percent: What's the difference

Mass-volume percent (% m/V, "weight-volume") shows how many grams of substance are contained in 100 mL of final solution:

% m/V = m(solute), g / V(solution), mL × 100

This is commonly used in medicine, microbiology, and pharmaceutics: "0.9% NaCl" in saline means 0.9 g of salt per 100 mL. Unlike chemistry problems, percentage concentration in medical prescriptions almost always means m/V. Such a solution is prepared differently: transfer the weighed amount to a volumetric flask, dissolve in part of the water, and make up to the mark with water. For accurate solutions, use a class A flask, and choose the volume and stopper according to the rules for choosing a volumetric flask.

How much these quantities differ can be seen with one solution. 10% NaCl by mass is 10 g salt and 90 g water, total 100 g. The density of such a solution at 20 °C is about 1.071 g/mL, so its volume is 100 / 1.071 = 93.4 mL, and the concentration in mass-volume percent is 10 / 93.4 × 100 = 10.7%. The higher the concentration and density, the greater the discrepancy. So when asked how to make a 10% solution according to a procedure, first answer which percent is meant.

Mass and volume have different dimensions, so weight-volume percentages are not formally a fraction, and always state in the lab notebook which percent is meant.

Common student mistakes

  • Dissolving the substance in 100 g of water. 5 g of salt in 100 g of water gives 4.76%, not 5%. The solution mass includes the substance.
  • Forgetting water of crystallization. Weighing the hydrate as if it were the anhydrous salt gives a solution 1.6–2.7 times weaker than needed, depending on the hydrate.
  • Substituting the percentage instead of the fraction. A 5% concentration goes into the formula as 0.05: 5 × 200 = 1000 g instead of 0.05 × 200 = 10 g. If the weighed amount is larger than the solution mass, the error is here.
  • Confusing mass fraction and mass-volume percent. Making a "10% solution" in a volumetric flask when the procedure calls for mass fraction, or vice versa.
  • Measuring the concentrate by volume without accounting for density. 75 g of 20% NaCl solution is 65.3 mL, not 75 mL.
  • Returning excess reagent to the bottle and contaminating it. Discard the excess or collect it in a separate container.
  • Not accounting for reagent purity. For technical reagents with an assay of, say, 95%, divide the weighed amount by 0.95.

Frequently asked questions

Can I measure water with a cylinder instead of weighing?

Yes, for educational and most working solutions. At 20 °C, 1 mL of water weighs 0.998 g, so 190 mL of water is 189.6 g, a difference of 0.2%. If high accuracy is needed, add water directly on the balance.

How to make a solution with a mass fraction if you need a volume of solution, not a mass?

Look up the density of the solution of the required concentration in a reference book and convert volume to mass: m(solution) = V × ρ. For example, 500 mL of 10% NaCl with density 1.071 g/mL weighs 535.5 g, so you need 53.55 g of salt and 481.95 g of water.

How does percentage concentration differ from molarity?

Percentage concentration is grams of substance per 100 g of solution, while molarity is moles of substance per 1 L of solution. You can convert one to the other only if you know the solution's density and the molar mass of the substance.

Should the reagent be dried before weighing?

Anhydrous reagents for standard solutions (Na₂CO₃, which absorbs moisture noticeably, and NaCl) are dried in an oven and cooled in a desiccator before preparation. Hydrates are not dried: making a solution from a hydrate relies on the exact composition of the crystals, and heating removes water of crystallization, making the conversion invalid.

How to convert mass-volume percent to mass fraction?

Divide it by the density of the solution in g/mL: w, % = % m/V / ρ. For saline 0.9% m/V with density about 1.005 g/mL, the mass fraction is 0.9 / 1.005 = 0.896%. For dilute aqueous solutions the difference is small; for concentrated ones it is noticeable.

To prepare a solution with the correct mass fraction, keep four rules in mind: find the mass of the solute by multiplying the fraction by the mass of the solution, treat water as the difference, recalculate a crystalline hydrate through the ratio of molar masses, and for volumetric methods first clarify which percentage you need—mass or mass-by-volume. Before the lab, write the calculation in your notebook, verify it by substituting back into w = m(solute) / m(solution), and only then head to the balance.
